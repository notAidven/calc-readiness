## Notes before authoring

**Five new nodes**, joint-smallest in the run alongside eras two, three and five, and this is
the first era where the island should read as a *city* rather than a settlement — twenty-one
buildings standing.

**Mortar and dressed stone are this era's signature move**, and they are deliberately withheld
from the three eras before it: era one is lashed timber and rammed earth, era two mud brick,
era three corbelled mass. So the first true arch and the first ashlar wall belong here.

**The risk to watch is ornament.** This is the first era where decorative language is genuinely
period-appropriate at scale, and `../../City_Art_Bible.md` §3 still requires one dominant shape
plus one accent, legible at full zoom-out. **A colonnade reads at zoom-out; a frieze does not.**

## 1. The era in one line, and its signature move

**One line:** a small port city built to a line, in cut stone, out of one bay repeated.

**The signature move — the repeated bay in stone.** The arch and the colonnade, and what
matters about them is that they are the same idea: **a single module counted along a
wall.** A colonnade is a bay repeated in the vertical, an arcade is a bay repeated with
its head closed, and either way the building is now a number of somethings rather than a
shape with holes in it. This is the first era in the run where **the decoration and the
structure are the same object** — the columns are holding the roof up, the arches are
carrying the wall over the openings, and removing the ornament would remove the building.
Era two's course and era three's fitting were both decorative consequences of a structural
fact; here the two collapse into one another.

It also passes the zoom-out test that the notes above set, which is why it is the right
signature rather than merely a period-correct one. **A colonnade reads at distance because
it is rhythm, and rhythm survives being small.** A frieze on the same wall does not
survive it, and the era should spend its ornament budget accordingly.

**Why it suits this era specifically.** This is the era the island stops being a place
that grew and becomes a place that was **set out** — the paving is laid to a camber, the
shoreline runs the whole way round in coursed stone, the trees stand in a line. A repeated
bay is that same discipline applied to a wall, and it is what makes twenty-one buildings
on one small island read as a city rather than as a collection: the module is shared, so
the buildings agree with each other even when they are doing different jobs.

**The tally descendant: the carved inscription.** Notches on a post became impressed clay,
then the stamped weight, and here the mark is **cut** — letters and numerals incised into
a panel of stone, with the shadow inside the cut doing the work rather than any pigment.
It is the most durable link in the motif's chain and the first one that is unmistakably
*writing* rather than counting, which is the right moment for that step: this is the era
that can afford a mason to spend a day on twenty characters. The stamped weight survives
in the market and the clay tablet in the workshop, but the inscription is the version that
is meant to outlast the people who cut it.

## 2. Materials

Four again, and after three eras of earth this is the set that changes the island's
colour.

| Material | Read | Notes for modelling |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Dressed ashlar limestone, laid in lime mortar** | The era's signature and the primary body. Pale, flat-faced, with fine regular joints | The joint is the material: coursed, thin, and level, so the wall reads as assembled from equal blocks rather than piled from found ones. Marble belongs inside this material as an accent — a threshold, a column drum, an inscription panel — and never as a whole body |
| **Terracotta roof tile** | Every roof. Warm, faintly ridged, and the era's one strong colour | Pan-and-cover tiling with an ordered ridge line and antefixes at the eaves. This is where era one's terracotta body hue survives, demoted from wall to roof |
| **Lime plaster and stucco** | Upper storeys, interiors of colonnades, the cheaper walls | A skin over rubble or brick where ashlar is not affordable, kept close to the body hue and just off-white. It should read as a surface with nothing to hide, not as a render pretending to be stone |
| **Timber roof truss** | The span over every wide roof, seen at gable ends and under eaves | The one place the era admits it is still working in wood. A triangulated truss is the structural invention that lets a hall be wide, and its end should be visible in a pediment |

**Explicitly absent, because they are later eras' signature moves:** the pointed arch and
the buttress, which the Medieval era owns; the timber frame expressed on the outside of a
wall, also Medieval, and the reason this era's stucco must never be divided into panels by
visible studs; the dome on a drum, which is the Renaissance's whole announcement; window
glass at any size, also Renaissance, so every opening here is a shutter, a grille or a
void; and structural iron, which waits for the Industrial era.

to spend it is on the transition itself: every earlier building visibly gains a straight
edge here, and the moment a wall's joints go thin and level the whole island looks
intentional.

## 3. Structural language

**Buildings stand up by geometry, and the geometry is on the outside.** Era three carried
its loads in mass and stepped them inward course by course. Here a load is gathered into a
column, taken across an opening by an arch, and spread by a truss — every one of which is
a thing you can see and count.

- **The colonnade and the arcade.** The era's structural spine, repeated at a constant
  spacing, and the source of both its silhouette and its ornament. Where a building needs
  to be more important it gets more bays or taller ones, never a different shape.
- **The true arch.** Voussoirs and a keystone, semicircular and no other curve. Openings
  can now be as wide as a room, which changes what a store or a hall can be.
- **The vault**, sparingly. A barrel vault over a bay of a warehouse, a half-dome over a
  niche. Nothing that needs a buttress, because the buttress is Medieval.
- **The trussed roof.** Triangulated timber under tile, which is what makes a single-span
  conical or pitched roof possible over a wide plan, and what makes the pediment an honest
  shape rather than a decorated one.
- **True symmetry, and an axis.** For the first time a building has a front in the sense
  of a designed approach — but the camera orbits a full circle, so **symmetry must be
  worked in both directions**: two matching sides and a legible back, never a decorated
  face and a blank rear.
- **The peristyle.** A colonnade turned inward round a court, which is how this era does
  an interior open space and how the dwelling chain's shared yard survives the transition.
- **Ground paved.** Fitted slabs laid to a camber, with a raised kerb and a gutter. Era
  three's metalled gravel is gone from the main routes.

**Heights are stated as an order, and this guide deliberately gives no world-unit targets.**
Every stage is contain-fit to its plot by **one uniform scale**, so the same model stands at
two different heights on two different plots and a number is not something the person
authoring one can aim at. The **ranking** is what an era owes, and it survives re-authoring.

**This era's order, tallest first:** the harbour tower, still standing clear; then the tholos
market hall, close behind it for the first time, because a colonnade lifts a roof; then the
public hearth, wide but now genuinely monumental; then the courtyard block, which is the
first dwelling in the run to be among the tall things rather than among the low ones; then
the market colonnade; then the portico; and the paved court lowest. **The era as a whole is a
step taller than era three**, and the interesting movement is the dwelling's, which climbs
further here than anything else. Nothing comes close to the 24-unit ceiling; only era ten's
mast is authored to approach it. **The paved court has not grown** — a basin and a line of
trees stand in it, and the ground under them is as flat as era one's swept earth.

## 4. Ornament and visible technology

**Ornament level: moderate for the first time, and the whole of the increase goes into the
order.** The column, its capital, the entablature above it and the pediment that closes a
gable — a system rather than a set of decisions, which is why it can be repeated
twenty-one times without becoming noise. Everything else stays thin: a moulded string
course, a cornice with a real shadow under it, antefixes along an eaves line, and the
inscription panel.

**What to leave out, and this is the section's real content.** The frieze, the relief
carving, the fluting, the acroterion, the painted architrave. All of them are
period-correct and none of them reads at the distance the camera actually holds, so they
cost detail and return nothing. **Spend on the profile of the cornice instead** — a shadow
line survives zoom-out, which is the one test that matters. Where a building needs to look
richer, give it a deeper cornice or another bay, not more surface.

>

>
> **Nothing here moves on that ruling's authority.** It was asked of era six, and era four's
> ornament budget is a different argument: this era spends its whole increase on the order, and a
> large medallion is not obviously something the Classical era should be given. What is owed is a
> decision, not an edit made by a passing agent.

**Visible technology, and the era is generous with it:** the treadwheel crane, the timber
scaffold with its putlogs, arch centring, the lime-slaking pit, the plumb and the square,
the surveyed line itself — visible in the paving and the tree planting — the aqueduct spur
on low arches, the public basin running continuously, the tile kiln, and the loaded cart
on a paved street.

**Nothing that implies the pointed arch, the exposed frame, the dome on a drum, or the
pane.** Curves are semicircles, walls are unbroken by studwork, roofs are pitched or conical
and **never carried on a drum**, and every opening is void, shuttered or grilled. Three later
eras are each waiting for one of those, and each of them arrives as a shock only if this era
holds the line. **The drum is the withheld half and the dome alone is not** — §2's banner
sets the boundary, and the Baths' caldarium sits inside it: a low dome springing straight off
its own walls, no drum beneath it and no lantern over it.

## The island (D-ERA26)

The Bronze Age cut the first stone edge. This era **finishes** it, and imposes geometry on
everything behind it.

**The step from era three, and it is the largest single ground change in the run.** Era three
levelled and metalled: rammed gravel and stone chips, kerbed, with bare earth and terraced
fields between. **Era four PAVES.** Loose aggregate becomes fitted stone, and the moment it does
the island stops having *surfaces* and starts having **a floor** — one continuous plane that
runs under everything and does not change where a building stops. **That is the read at the
advance: the ground has become a single object.**

**Ground.** Paved. Properly paved, for the first time: **fitted stone slabs** across the
central ground and the main routes, laid to a **camber** so water runs off, with a raised kerb
and a gutter beside it. The paving is worn smooth in the middle of the routes and rougher at
the edges. Where the ground is not paved it is **compacted and levelled** rather than natural.

**The paving is a MODULE, and that is the era's signature move applied to the ground.** §1 makes
this era *"a single module counted along a wall"*; the floor is the same discipline laid flat.
So the slabs are **to a size** — a small number of sizes, not one, but a set anybody could
count — laid to a **regular bond** rather than fitted crazy-paving fashion, with the joint lines
running through and lining up across the open ground rather than stopping at each building.
**The eye should be able to follow one joint line across the whole square.** That is what makes
a paved island read as *set out* rather than merely *hard*.

**And the fields go, which is the quiet half of this advance.** Era two broke the plots and era
three regularised them; era four is a **port city** and its land is worth more as floor than as
field. The terraces survive as **level changes retained in ashlar**, the field walls are gone,
and cultivation has left the island for the mainland the grain arrives from. *(The era's own
`store` civic is **The Grain Dock** — the name says where this era's grain lands rather than
where it grows.)* **A player should notice the fields have gone.**

**Shoreline.** The retaining wall of era three runs **the whole way round**, and it is now
**dressed ashlar** — squared, coursed, laid in lime mortar, with a moulded coping along the top.
Steps descend to the water at intervals. A **breakwater** of large blocks reaches out to make a
proper enclosed harbour basin, and the mole has a small lighthouse-like tower at its head. There
is no natural shore left anywhere on the seaward side; only behind the buildings does a strip of
the old bank survive, and it looks like an oversight. **Era three's dry-laid rubble is still
visible in one or two places under the new facing**, and that is an authored survival rather than
stratification — this guide names it, which is the test.

**Vegetation.** Deliberate and formal. **Trees in rows** — a colonnade of them along the main
route, evenly spaced, trunks limbed up to the same height, **which is the paving's module
carried into the planting** and should read as the same decision. Clipped hedging around
courtyards. Vines on frames. Everything wild has been pushed to the very margin or removed; even
the "natural" planting is placed. The reeds are gone. **Era three's orchard in rough rows becomes
an avenue at one spacing**, and the difference between *roughly in rows* and *at one spacing* is
the whole of this era.

**Where boats land.** A **stone harbour**: quays on two sides of the basin, bollards cut from
single blocks, stone-built warehouses opening directly onto the wharf, a slipway with dressed
stone sides, and cargo — jars, timber, blocks — stacked in orderly lines under a colonnaded
shelter. Larger vessels moored alongside rather than beached.

**Water.** Inside the breakwater the water is **still and green-dark**, deep enough at the quay
face that the bottom is no longer visible — a change worth making explicit, because era one's
"see the bottom" shallowness is now gone for good. Outside the breakwater it stays paler and
livelier.

**Signs of habitation at the margins.** An aqueduct spur arriving on low arches and discharging
into a stone tank. A public fountain where a route meets the paving. Statuary on plinths at the
harbour head. Rubbish is now *collected* rather than middened — bins, and a paved yard behind
the warehouses. **A carved inscription set into the paving or a retaining face**, which is §1's
tally descendant landing on the ground as well as on the buildings.

**Against the roads (D-ERA30).** **This era is the one where the ground and the road are most at
risk of being the same decision made twice**, because the paving runs under both and the routes
are *"worn smooth in the middle"* rather than differently made. The island owns the **open
paved ground**; D-ERA30 owns the **route tile**. The relation, stated once: era four is the
first era in which **the road is not a different material from the ground it crosses** — it is
the same slab, distinguished by the **kerb and the gutter beside it** and by wear polish down
its middle. Whoever writes era four's surfacing row should express it as *kerb + carriage +
gutter* against a ground that is already stone, not as a new paving. **If a future row makes
era four's road a different material, this section is wrong and should be corrected rather than
both being left to disagree.**

**What the era's own absent list rules out of the ground.** §2 withholds the **pointed arch and
the buttress**, the **external timber frame**, the **dome on a drum**, **window glass at any
size**, and **structural iron**. Most of that is architectural, but two reach the ground and one
near-miss is worth naming:

- **No VISIBLE iron on the ground**, so **no iron grating, no iron drain cover and no iron kerb
  edging**. A gutter discharges through a **carved stone spout or a slotted stone channel**, and
  the drain is an open channel rather than a covered one. Era seven is where the ground grows
  iron.

  > **CORRECTED 2026-08-01 — the clause *"and no iron tie in a wall"* is struck, and the three
  > that remain are re-headed by what they have in common.** The tie was false twice over.
  > **Factually:** the Parthenon and the Erechtheion set double-T clamps in their masonry, and
  > Roman vault tie-bars are concealed in the core from Trajan onward, so a Classical building
  > with no iron tie in it is not the period this era draws. **And decisively for a render:
  > those elements are concealed by design.** A clamp is bedded in a joint and a tie-bar is
  > buried in the core; neither shows on any elevation at any zoom, so forbidding them buys the
  > silhouette nothing while making the description untrue. The other three clauses stand
  > unchanged, because each of them names iron a player would *see* — which is the rule the list
  > was always about, and the heading now says so rather than leaving it to be inferred from §2's
  > word *"structural"*.
- **No glass**, so nothing on this island is lit through anything and nothing on the ground
  reflects a lit interior at night.
- **The near-miss: the arch is NOT absent here — it is this era's own signature.** The aqueduct
  on low arches above is correct and is the one place the ground-level scene may use it. It is
  era five's **pointed** arch that is forbidden, so keep every arch a clean semicircle.

**Contrast against the buildings, and THIS ERA IS THE FIRST REAL COLLAPSE RISK IN THE RUN.**
Body hue **dressed ashlar limestone `#D2C8AA`** — and the ground is now **the same dressed
limestone, laid flat**. **The buildings and the floor are literally the same material for the
first time**, which is historically right and visually dangerous: a pale wall rising out of a
pale floor has no silhouette at the base, and silhouette-first legibility is §0 constraint 7.
The 2026-07-30 value makes this *harder rather than easier* and is honest about it: `#D2C8AA`
is ΔE76 **12.37** from §1.1's parchment ground and only **2.98** from the deep ground
`#D9CCB2`, the tightest body-to-ground reading in the run. The three resolutions below are
therefore load-bearing rather than precautionary.

**Three things resolve it and all three are already in the era's own material list:**

1. **The paving must sit a clear value step BELOW the wall.** It is horizontal, so it takes the
   top-face lightening in the three-tone model and would otherwise come out *lighter* than the
   wall it abuts. Author it greyer, cooler and more worn than a wall face, and let the gutters
   and joint lines carry a darker line.
2. **The terracotta roof accent `#B4674A` is what actually separates a building from the ground
   at zoom-out**, and it is the only strong colour in the era. Era four should not be shy with
   roof area.
3. **The kerb and the moulded coping give every edge a dark line**, which is the base shadow the
   flat lighting model will not supply.

**Two of the three older generations help here and the third does not**, which is a change
from what this paragraph said before 2026-07-30. Era one's terracotta `#C98A5E` and era
three's ochre `#8A6E33` are warm mid-tones standing on cool pale stone, so they read well
against it and get darker as they get older, which is the right way round. **Era two no
longer does**: its redesigned straw-tempered brick `#D9C288` is L\* 79.1 against this era's
80.7, so a Neolithic wall and a Classical wall are the same lightness on this floor and are
separated by **chroma alone** — C\* 32.2 against 16.3, ΔE76 16.02 and **11.70 in situ**
against a floor of 6.72. It clears, comfortably, but it clears on the one axis the paving
cannot help with. **Era four is the last era where the accumulated city is free**, and era
two is where that stopped being free by a whole era.

**The read:** a small port city that has decided the island's natural shape was a first draft.
Everything is level, edged, and to a line.

## 6. The three construction stages

All twenty-one buildings get three models each. The stage names are fixed in code
(`buildingAssets.ts:41`), and the era's job is to make the sequence read as *this era's*
way of building — which in this era means that the building is designed before it is
begun.

| Stage | Engine states | Classical read |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **`site`** | `not_started`, `preview` | **A full-size setting-out drawing scratched into a paved slab** — the plan of the building, incised at actual size on the ground it will stand on. Beside it a lime-slaking pit with the lime still hot and steaming, and blocks numbered where they lie, waiting for the course they belong to. Flat, ankle-height at most, and unmistakably *designed* |
| **`construction`** | `teaching`, `practice` | A timber scaffold with **putlogs let into the wall** itself, a **treadwheel crane standing on the wall head** and lifting by lewis iron, and **arch centring in timber under every opening**. The crane climbs with the building, so it is always at the top of whatever exists |
| **`built`** | `unfinished`, `complete`, full mastery | The finished building as described in §5 and in the roster. `advanced` (mastery ≥ 2) and full mastery both reuse this model — there is no separate gilded version, and mastery rides above it as stars (`buildingAssets.ts:28-31`) |

**The setting-out floor is the strongest site stage in the run, and it is the era's whole
thesis in one image.** Era one stripped turf and drove stakes, era two laid out green
bricks to dry, era three pegged string lines and hung a plumb-bob — each of them a step
towards measurement. Here the building exists **at full size, drawn, before a single block
is set**, which is the difference between a settlement that builds and a city that plans.
It also reads perfectly from directly above, which is close to the angle the camera holds.

**Arch centring is the construction stage's dating device**, and it should be everywhere:
a timber former under each opening, visibly temporary, visibly the reason the arch above
it is possible. A player who has watched three eras of lintels will read a curve of new
timber as an announcement.

**A note on the lewis iron, so it does not look like a contradiction.** §2 withholds
**structural** iron for the Industrial era. Iron as a tool and a lifting cramp is
period-correct and belongs here; iron as a beam, a column or a truss does not. The
distinction is load-bearing in both senses: a crane hook may be iron, a roof may not.

   ashlar has yards full of sawn stone whether or not one of them is a skill building.)*
2. **§2's "Explicitly absent" list is a hard boundary.** No pointed arch, no buttress, no exposed
   timber frame, no dome on a drum, **no window glass at any size**, and no structural iron. A
   word problem may not contain them.
3. **Theming follows the ERA, not the domain.** Polynomials is split across three eras; these
   five nodes are framed Classical and the other three are not this guide's business.

**The era's context palette — six reusable settings, from this guide's own props:** the **stone
yard** and its sawn blocks; the **paved court** laid to a camber with a kerb and gutter; the
**colonnade** as a counted row of bays; the **bonded warehouse** and its vaulted bays; the
**aqueduct spur** and public basin; and the **harbour mole** with its loaded carts.

**One watch that applies to every row, because this era's mathematics invites it.** Polynomials
are areas here — that is why the era was chosen (*"geometric algebra, areas as products"*). A
rectangle of ground genuinely *is* the era's own way of thinking about a product, so the framings
below use it freely. What they must not do is let the **building** become the diagram: the mason's
workshop hosts POL.1, it does not illustrate it.

| Node | The mathematics | Classical framings (2 to 3 per node) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **POL.1** — add, subtract, multiply polynomials | combining like terms; products of binomials | (1) **A course of ashlar** is `a` blocks long and `b` courses high, and widening the wall by two blocks while raising it by one is a product of two sums. (2) **Two orders of cut stone combined** — one yard's tally and another's, added term by term, with lintels never added to drums. (3) **A colonnade's stone** as bays × (shaft + capital + base), multiplied out. |
| **POL.2** — factor: GCF, trinomials, grouping, perfect squares, difference of squares | reading a product back to its sides | (1) **A paved court of known area, laid out to find its sides** — the mason knows how many slabs he has and needs the rectangle. (2) **The common measure** every block in an order shares, taken out first: a GCF is the largest module that divides the whole job. (3) **A square court with a square pool cut out of it** is the difference of two squares, and the leftover paving is the two rectangles you get by rearranging the L. |
| **POL.3** — solve quadratics by factoring and the square-root property | when a product is zero; taking a root | (1) **Sizing a square court to a required area** — the square-root property, directly, in a job the era does constantly. (2) **A margin of paving around a square basin**: the outer square is known, the margin is known, and the basin's side falls out. (3) **A cut that leaves nothing over** — a length divided into two parts whose product is fixed, which is where factoring earns its place. |
| **POL.4** — completing the square; the quadratic formula | rearranging to a square; the general solution | (1) **Literally completing a square**: an L-shaped piece of ground with the corner missing, and the mason works out the block that squares it off. This is the era's own name for the method and the framing is not a coincidence. (2) **A barrel-vaulted bay's span and rise** given its area of opening. (3) **When the ground will not square** — a case with no whole-number answer, motivating the general formula over the trick. |
| **POL.8** — polynomial long and synthetic division | dividing one polynomial by another | (1) **The graded rules and rods on the workshop wall** — dividing a length by a measure and recording what is left over, which is what a remainder is. (2) **Apportioning a load of stone** across a known number of bays, largest unit first, then the next, exactly as long division proceeds. (3) **Checking a division by rebuilding it** — quotient times divisor plus remainder returns the original order, which is the yard's own audit. |

**Do not reach for the tally motif here.** §1 records the era's tally descendant as the **carved
inscription**, and it is deliberately *writing* rather than counting. A lesson that frames
arithmetic as notching a post is two eras out of date.
