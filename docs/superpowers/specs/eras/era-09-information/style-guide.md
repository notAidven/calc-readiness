## 1. The era in one line, and its signature move

**One line:** a sealed, quiet town of glass and panel, with all of its machinery buried and
nothing on any wall to say how it was made.

**The signature move — the sealed reflective skin.** No visible joint that is not part of a grid,
no visible service, no visible structure. A building becomes a surface: a continuous plane of glass
and panel drawn tight over a frame nobody sees, meeting the ground with a shadow gap rather than a
base. **This is the first era whose buildings do not explain themselves.** Era eight hid its frame
but still showed a slab edge and a board mark; era nine shows a reflection of the sky and the next
building along. The move belongs here because it is what the period's construction actually was —
unitised panels made in a factory, craned into place, sealed — and because it is the visual form of
the era's premise, that the important machinery has moved where you cannot look at it.

**The tally descendant: the display panel — and this is era one's own prediction landing
exactly.** Era one wrote that *notches on a post become a chalked slate becomes a printed ledger
becomes a display panel*, and here the display panel is. Not a screen showing a graph, and per
D-ERA8 never a screen showing a building's own lesson: a small illuminated matrix panel by a
door, on a totem, above a counter, showing a number that changes. It is the fourth and last
predicted step in the motif's chain, nine eras after the first notch was cut, and it should be
treated as a scheduled arrival rather than an invention.

## 2. Materials

Four, and the relationship between them is the era's real subject.

| Material | Read | Notes for modelling |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Unitised curtain-wall glass** | The body material. Flat, reflective, faintly cool, banded by a fine grid | Comes as storey-high units with a visible mullion and transom grid and a spandrel panel at each floor. The grid is the only texture; it must stay regular, because an irregular one reads as an earlier era |
| **Anodised and composite panel** | Matte, close-toned, seamless at a distance | Rainscreen panels with a narrow open joint. The joint pattern is the whole of the detail, so it wants to be set out deliberately rather than tiled at random |
| **Exposed structural steel** | Slender, pale, deliberate | Only where it is being *expressed* — a canopy, an atrium screen, a mast. Steel that looks structural by accident belongs to era seven |
| **Engineered stone cladding** | Dry, pale, precise, ground level only | Granite or reconstituted stone on podiums and retaining faces, in large thin panels with tight joints. It is the era's one nod to weight, and it is a facing, never a wall |

**Explicitly absent, because they are era ten's signature moves:** emissive surface used as a
whole face, exposed conduit and cable tray run across an elevation, and the deck carried over the
older ground. What era nine *stops* withholding is **the unitised curtain wall and the composite
panel** — the two things that let a building be a skin rather than a wall.

**The era's real absence is not a material at all: visible hand-work of any kind.** Nothing on an
era-nine building shows that a person made it. No board mark, no tooled stone, no laid course, no
lashing, no rivet, no brush stroke — every surface arrives finished from somewhere else and is
fixed by a bracket you cannot see. Eight eras of buildings carried the evidence of their own making
on the outside; this is the era that stops, and that is the absence the era is *about*. It also
sets up era ten's reply, which is to make the fixings visible again on purpose.

## 3. Structural language

**Buildings stand up somewhere you cannot see.** The structure is a frame, as in era eight, but
now the skin is held clear of it, so nothing about the load path reaches the outside. The
vocabulary is:

- **The unitised grid.** A regular mullion and transom pattern wrapping every face, including the
  ones nobody would have bothered with in an earlier era. There is no back.
- **Podium and tower.** A wide clad base with the taller element set on it, the base holding the
  ground and the tower ignoring it. It is the era's standard composition.
- **The clear span.** One roof over one undivided floor, with the plant hidden in the depth above
  the soffit — the move that makes the concourse possible.
- **The shadow gap.** Skin stops short of the ground and of the parapet, so a building reads as a
  wrapped object rather than as something resting on anything.
- **Rooftop plant as silhouette.** Chillers, screens and louvred enclosures are the only things
  breaking a flat top edge, and they give era-nine roofs their whole profile.
- **Ramps rather than steps** wherever the ground changes, because everything is designed to be
  got into.

**Heights are stated as an order, and this guide deliberately gives no world-unit targets.**
Every stage is contain-fit to its plot by **one uniform scale**, so the same model stands at two
different heights on two different plots and a number is not something the person authoring one
can aim at. The **ranking** is what an era owes, and it survives re-authoring.

**This era's order, tallest first:** the comms mast, still clear of everything; then the office
tower, and this is the closest anything roofed has come to the mast since era six's dome; then
the residential block and the data hall, effectively level with one another, with the transit
concourse just under them — **so the hall stays out of the top two, as it has since era eight**;
then the sealed laboratory; then the research lab; and the dish yard lowest. **The era climbs
one more step on era eight** and the movement is all in the podium: everything now stands on
structure, so the base of each building starts higher than the ground it covers. Nothing here
reaches the 24-unit ceiling; only era ten's mast is authored to approach it, and **the ceiling is
a cap rather than a target**, because anything taller is silently squashed.

**Aspect ratio is where the era's extremes live, and it belongs to the models rather than to the
plots.** The mast should be authored several times taller than it is wide — the most extreme
proportion the run has reached — and because a contain-fit preserves that proportion on any plot,
the proportion is the whole of what makes it read as a mast. The same lever run the other way is
what keeps the research lab long and the dish yard flat.

## 4. Ornament and visible technology

**Ornament level: zero, and unlike era eight the zero is not a statement, it is an assumption.**
Era eight was proud of having no ornament. Era nine simply does not have the category. What
patterns a face is the panel joint and the glazing grid; what interrupts it is the signage band
and the entrance; and that is the entire vocabulary.

**Signage carries what an earlier era's ornament carried.** A back-lit band across a fascia, a
logotype standing off a panel, a wayfinding totem at the plot edge, a display panel at the door.
The era identifies its buildings almost entirely by the graphics fixed to them, which is worth
watching against constraint 9: signage may say what a building is, but nothing a learner must
know can live in art alone.

**Visible technology, and there is deliberately very little.** The satellite dish, the comms mast,
the rooftop chiller, the louvred plant screen, the flush duct marker, the display panel. Everything
else is inside or underground. **The era's paradox is that it is the most technological era so far
and the one that shows the least of it** — the trace of a data hall is a shimmer of warm air.

**Where D-ERA8 bites here.** Exponential growth is this era's mathematical read and the city
already expresses it, so no building needs a curve on it and none may have one. A logarithmic sweep
on a façade, a graph etched into a spandrel, a parapet that doubles at each bay — the same mistake
in different clothes.

## The island (D-ERA26)

The era that **hides its infrastructure**. Everything the Atomic Age proudly showed in concrete
is now buried, decked over or planted on top of, and the ground becomes a surface with things
underneath it.

**The step from era eight, and this is the QUIETEST advance in the run at material level — so it
has to be authored on the axes where it is loud.** Both grounds are hard, pale and flat, and an
artist who changes only the material will produce two islands a player cannot tell apart. **The
change is grain, not substance**, and the folder has already ruled that grain carries: era one's
guide separates its hall from its dwelling on *"one round roof against several small flat ones"*
and records that **grain survives distance better than height does**. That precedent is what this
advance rests on.

**Stated as the one sentence to design from: era eight POURED its ground and era nine LAYS it.**
A monolithic plane divided by a handful of wide structural joints becomes **thousands of small
precise units in a deliberate banded pattern**. Every other difference below follows from that
one, and it is the same change era nine's buildings make — §1's *unitised panels made in a
factory, craned into place, sealed*. **The wall and the floor are the same idea in this era**,
which is why the ground genuinely does carry the age here even though it does so quietly.

**Ground.** **Engineered paving on structure.** Precise unit paving — large-format slabs, granite
setts in banded patterns, resin-bound gravel in the softer areas — **laid over podiums and
service voids rather than on soil**. **Tactile paving, inspection covers, uplighters and flush
drainage channels** everywhere, and **the covers are part of the pattern rather than apologised
for** — squared to the module, aligned to the joints, in the same material family. Ground levels
change by **ramp** as often as by step, because everything is designed to be accessible.

**The ground IS the era's premise, and that is worth saying plainly.** An era whose whole
identity is that the important machinery has moved where you cannot look at it has a floor that
is **the lid over it**. Everything a service needs — access, drainage, power, data — is present
as a **flush, squared, deliberately patterned detail** rather than as a thing avoided. **The
inspection cover is this island's most characteristic object.**

**The Atomic concrete survives underneath and shows at the retaining faces**, which this section
names, making it an authored survival rather than stratified ground (D-ERA33).

**Shoreline.** **Softened on purpose, and that is the era's move — and it is where this advance
is LOUD.** The vertical concrete revetment is still structurally there, but it is now dressed as
a **stepped and terraced edge**: broad timber-decked platforms, planted terraces stepping down
toward the water, occasional reinstated **shingle and reed margins** built into engineered
pockets. **It is the first time since the Bronze Age that anything shelves toward the water** —
six eras of vertical faces ending — and it is a *constructed* shelve, deliberate and detailed,
not a survival. **If beat four of the advance into era nine shows one thing, it should be this**,
because the shoreline change is large where the ground change is fine.

**Vegetation.** **Lush and layered, and mostly on structure** — and this is the advance's second
loud axis. Era eight's planting was **mown grass, standard trees at regular spacing and geometric
beds**; era nine's is **multi-stem trees in generous groups rather than rows, ornamental grasses
in drifts, sedum and wildflower on roofs and podium tops, reed and iris in the water margins**.
Municipal tidiness becomes deliberate looseness. **Deliberately naturalistic in *form* while
being entirely artificial in *fact*** — the planting is in engineered soil over waterproofing,
and the "wild" margins are managed. **Between the shoreline and the planting, era nine's island
changes more than its ground does, and its guide should be honest about that rather than leaning
on the paving to do work it cannot.**

**Where boats land.** Understated. A **floating pontoon** with an aluminium gangway, glass
balustrades, a small covered waiting shelter. Water transport is a service among others, and the
landing looks like a transit stop rather than a harbour.

**Water.** **Managed and clear.** Blue-green, clean, with visible reed beds and planted margins
performing filtration in full view — the era likes its environmental systems to be legible.
**Level is controlled, so the waterline is constant and there is no tide mark** — the one place
this era's hidden machinery is admitted, since a constant level implies a pump nobody can see.

**Signs of habitation at the margins.** Wayfinding totems. CCTV and lighting columns with
multiple heads. Cycle docks. Recycling banks in matched enclosures. A small pumping station
disguised as a pavilion. Fibre and duct markers set flush into the paving. **A small illuminated
matrix panel on a totem showing a number that changes**, which is era one's own tally prediction
landing on schedule.

**Against the roads (D-ERA30).** Era nine is the era in which **the road stops being a different
surface and becomes a different part of the same surface** — the second time this relation has
inverted since era eight established asphalt-against-concrete. Vehicle routes here are **the same
unit paving in a different module and a different band**, flush with everything around them,
level, kerbless in places and separated by tactile and colour changes rather than by height.
**So the island and the road share a material and differ by module**, which is a genuinely new
relation and the one whoever writes era nine's surfacing row should express: *carriage band +
tactile margin + flush channel*, in the ground's own stone. **The painted marking era eight
introduced is nearly gone**, because this era organises by pattern rather than by paint.

**What the era's own absent list rules out of the ground, and one item needs a precise reading.**
§2 withholds **emissive surface used as a whole face**, **exposed conduit and cable tray run
across an elevation**, and **the deck carried over the older ground** — all three era ten's.

- **THE UPLIGHTERS ARE PERMITTED AND THE PRECISION MATTERS.** Era eight withholds *emissive
  surface of any kind*; era nine withholds it only **used as a whole face**. So the flush
  uplighters and the illuminated matrix panel above are **inside** this era's list where they
  would be outside era eight's — a real widening at exactly the right moment. **What is
  forbidden is any emissive surface large enough to be read as a lit plane**: no glowing paving
  band, no lit floor strip running a route, no wash of light across the ground. **Small, few,
  and never a surface that lights another surface.** Era ten owns light as material.
- **NO DECK, WHICH IS THE DISTINCTION THIS ERA HANDS TO ERA TEN AND MUST NOT SPEND.** Era nine's
  paving is **on structure** — over podiums and service voids — and era ten's is a **deck** you
  can see down through. Those are close enough to be confused and the difference is exact:
  **era nine's floor is solid and continuous and gives no view of what is under it**, however
  much is under it. **No grille, no grating you can see through, no gap.** The inspection covers
  are *covers*: they are closed. If a player can look down and see servicing, this is era ten.
- **No exposed conduit or cable tray**, at ground level as much as on a wall — hence the **flush
  fibre and duct markers**, which say where the service is without showing it, and are the era's
  whole attitude in one object.

**Contrast against the buildings.** Body hue **anodised grey-blue `#96A1AB`**, accent
**curtain-wall glass `#7FA0A8`**, on pale grey unit paving. **The risk is moderate rather than
acute** and it differs in kind from eras four, six and eight: the building is **cool and
reflective** rather than pale and matte, so it does not merge with a pale floor the way stucco
does — but it is also **the lowest-contrast body/accent pair in the run** (`#96A1AB` against
`#7FA0A8`), so the building has little internal contrast of its own to fall back on.

**What resolves it:**

1. **The planting, and it is doing three jobs in this era.** Lush layered green in generous
   groups is the only strongly non-grey field on the island, and by era nine it is the primary
   separator rather than an amenity.
2. **The shadow gap at the base.** §1's *meeting the ground with a shadow gap rather than a base*
   is the same dark ground-level line eras six and eight rely on, and it is the fourth
   consecutive pale-ish era to need one. **That is now a rule and not a coincidence.**
3. **Keep the paving warmer or darker than the body hue, not cooler.** Every pale era before this
   was told to go cooler than its wall; era nine's wall is already cool, so the instruction
   inverts — a **grey-brown granite** floor separates from `#96A1AB` where a blue-grey one
   would not.

**The accumulated city is now at its hardest, and this is the era to state the general rule
at.** Standing on era nine's ground: era four's limestone, era five's lime-wash, era six's
stucco and era eight's concrete (four pale), era seven's soot brick and era ten's dark composite
to come (dark), era one's terracotta, era two's mud brick and era three's ochre (warm mid), and
era nine's own cool grey-blue. **No single ground value separates all of them, and no later era's
ground can be chosen as though it only had to hold its own buildings.** The durable answer, which
eras six, eight and nine each arrived at independently: **the ground sits MID-VALUE and
LOW-CHROMA**, between the pale and dark families rather than at either end, and **each era's
buildings carry their own dark ground-level element** — kerb, frame, rusticated base, shadow gap
— so that separation never depends on the floor alone. **A ground at either extreme of value
serves one half of the city and abandons the other.**

**The read:** an island where everything works and almost nothing of the machinery is visible.
The Atomic Age built the platform; this era decorated the top of it and buried the rest.

## 6. The three construction stages

Every building gets three models. The stage names are fixed in code (`buildingAssets.ts:41`), and
the era's job is to make the sequence read as *this era's* way of building.

| Stage | Engine states | Information read |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **`site`** | `not_started`, `preview` | A hoarded plot, and **the hoarding carries a printed picture of the finished building.** Neat plywood or profiled-steel panels, a gate, a wheel wash. You cannot see in |
| **`construction`** | `teaching`, `practice` | **System scaffold in debris netting**, so the shape is deliberately illegible; a mast climber and a goods hoist on one face; a mobile crane beside the plot. The silhouette is hidden and that is the point |
| **`built`** | `unfinished`, `complete`, full mastery | The finished building as described in §5. `advanced` (mastery ≥ 2) and full mastery both reuse this model — there is no separate gilded version, and mastery rides above it as stars (`buildingAssets.ts:28-31`) |

**This is the first era in the run whose site shows you an image instead of the work, and that is
the whole of the stage.** Era one's site was the shape of the building scratched into the earth;
era nine's is a photograph of it on a fence. Every era between showed something real and partial —
stakes, a footing trench, a reinforcement cage — and this one shows the finished thing before it

## The advisor (D-ERA28)

**Oru, dressed and speaking as a network operations lead.** The learner's companion for the whole
Information Age, carrying the mini-lesson, the check-your-understanding prompt and the hint exactly
as he does in era one — because he *is* era one's advisor, in this era's clothes and this era's
voice.

**Who he is — THE REGISTER, which is per-era and is meant to be.** The person who watches the whole
system at once and thinks in **orders of magnitude** — who says "that is ten times last week" before
he says what the number is, because at this scale the exponent is the information and the digits are
detail. That is the era design's own reason for putting exponentials and logarithms here, and it is
a person as much as a subject. Calm, unhurried, and given to reaching for a comparison rather than a
figure.

**What he is wearing — THE DRESSING, and this era's absence is the hardest one to dress.** §2
records that the era's real absence is **visible hand-work of any kind** — nothing shows that a
person made it — and a character has to survive that without becoming a mannequin. So: a fine merino
crew-neck over plain trousers, everything **seamless-looking, machine-finished, and utterly without
a visible stitch, weave or wear mark**. Soft trainers. A **lanyard** with a blank white card, which
is the era's own way of saying a person is authorised without saying who they are. He carries a
**tablet**, held flat, showing §1's tally descendant — the **display panel**, a static readout
rather than a graph. It is his counterpart to Oru's staff, and the fact that it shows nothing
handmade is the point. **The one deliberate exception is a paper coffee cup**, because a figure
made entirely of finished surfaces reads as an asset and not a person, and one soft dented object
in his hand is the cheapest fix.

### The four poses

## Lesson framings

**What this section is.** The era folder carries lesson framing (team lead, 2026-07-29) — how a
lesson sets its mathematics inside the era's world. It replaces the retired island themes spec.

**This era's lessons are now FULLY written.** `content/lessons/d8/` holds `EL.1` through `EL.5`
(Anthony, PR #223, merged 2026-07-29) and `EL.6` (Anthony, 2026-07-30), so the era has **no
unauthored node left**. Every row below therefore describes framing that is in use rather than
framing on offer; where a row and the shipped pack disagree, **read the pack**.

`EL.6` was written from framing (1) below, the one this guide put first: a **capacity review** in a
**server hall** whose load doubles on a cadence, asking not how big the load gets but *when* it
reaches the ceiling. That question is what puts the unknown in the exponent, which is the node's
whole subject, so the framing carries the mathematics rather than decorating it. Framings (2) and
(3) are unspent and are the natural sources for practice prompts.

**Three rules the era imposes.**

1. **The framing comes from the era's WORLD, not the node's BUILDING** (D-ERA8). The dish yard
   hosts EL.6; a satellite dish is not a picture of a logarithm.
2. **§2's "Explicitly absent" list is a hard boundary** — no emissive surface used as a whole
   face, no exposed conduit or cable tray on an elevation, no deck over the older ground. And the
   larger absence: **nothing shows that a person made it.** A framing built on craft, wear or
   hand-finishing is the wrong era.
3. **Theming follows the ERA, not the domain.** This era is one whole domain, so nothing splits.

**The era's context palette — six reusable settings, from this guide's own props:** the **server
hall** and the load it carries; the **display panel** and its static readout; the **logistics
shed** and its dock doors in a rhythm; the **clean room** behind its airlock; the **dish yard**;
and **the campus green**, which is the only soft ground left in the era.

*(The **logistics shed** is kept deliberately after `EL.4` was re-authored as a laboratory, and rule
1 above is why: a framing is a fact about the era's **WORLD**, not about a node's building, and an
Information Age city plainly runs on distribution sheds whether or not a skill building is one. It
is better backed than it was — the civic **The Distribution Center** authors exactly that shed, with
its long dock face and its trailers, and `civic-buildings.md` is where the art for it now lives.
What would be wrong is the reverse: briefing the **art** for `EL.4` as a shed, which §5 no longer
authors.)*

**Orders of magnitude are this era's real subject and the framings should show it.** Doubling
transistors is the era design's own phrase. What makes a logarithm *useful* here is that it turns
a quantity nobody can picture into a number a person can say out loud, and every framing below
should be recognisable as that move rather than as an exercise in undoing an exponent.

| --- | --- | --- |
