## 1. The era in one line, and its signature move

**One line:** a confident, cleaned-up town of poured concrete and thin flat planes, built by
people who are certain the next fifty years will be better than the last fifty.

**The signature move — the thin flat plane.** A canopy floating clear of the wall on slender
posts, a spandrel band running unbroken across a front, a roof slab that reads at zoom-out as
a *line* rather than as a mass. **For the first time in the run a building's most memorable
element carries no load you can see.** Every era before this made its structure legible — post
and lintel, the arch, the buttress, the riveted iron frame — and era eight buries the frame in
the concrete and hangs a plane in front of it. The move belongs to this era and no other:
reinforced concrete is the first material on the island that can be thin and still span, so
the flat plane is not a taste applied to the period but the thing the material has just made
possible, for a century that measured everything and liked the answer to look tidy.

**The tally descendant: the dial and the punched card.** Era one predicted this motif's own
chain — *notches on a post become a chalked slate becomes a printed ledger becomes a display
panel* — and era eight is the step where the count leaves the surface. A notch, a chalk stroke
and a ruled ledger line are all marks a hand put on a thing you can touch; a dial is a count
read off an instrument, and a punched card is a count nobody can read at all. Dial boards
belong on ordinary buildings as era ornament, exactly as tally posts did — per D-ERA8 a dial
is never a gauge showing a building's own output.

## 2. Materials

Four, and no more. The era's restraint is industrial rather than poor.

| Material | Read | Notes for modelling |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Poured and precast concrete** | The body material, and the era's signature. Matte, pale, flat | Two finishes doing different jobs: board-marked in situ, which gives the only wall texture the era allows — vertical, coarse, directional — and smooth precast, which reads flatter and carries a visible panel-joint grid. Arrises are sharp; this is the first era whose corners are meant to look cut |
| **Structural steel** | Slender, dark, linear | Used where concrete would be clumsy: the lattice pylon, canopy posts, glazing mullions, handrails. Painted a dark neutral so it reads as a drawn line against a pale wall |
| **Plate glass in large sheets** | Continuous, faintly cool, weakly reflective | Comes in ribbons and full bays, never in small panes. In earlier eras glass was a hole in a wall; here it is a whole side of the building |
| **Anodised aluminium trim** | One thin bright line | Copings, fascias, glazing beads, lettering. The era's only bright metal, and it never becomes a mass |

**Explicitly absent, because they are later eras' signature moves:** composite rainscreen
cladding and the sealed, jointless skin (Information), emissive surface of any kind (Cyberpunk),
and exposed conduit and the deck laid over the ground (Cyberpunk). What era eight *stops*
withholding is its own whole kit: **reinforced concrete, the cantilever, plate glass and
anodised aluminium** were held back through seven eras so they could arrive together here.

**The era's biggest absence is a retirement rather than a reservation. Brick and timber leave
the walls entirely here, and never come back.** Brick had been a wall material for six eras and
timber for seven — timber stands in era one's first post, brick arrives with the Neolithic kiln —
and after era eight neither is the substance of a new building again. Both survive only as things
the era inherited: a preserved older wall, a paving band, a painted bench slat. That is era
eight's clearest statement about itself, because it makes this the first era that does not build
out of anything the island had before.

## 3. Structural language

**Buildings stand up inside themselves.** The frame is a reinforced concrete skeleton and you
never see it working; what you see is the frame's consequences — a regular bay, a floor that
oversails, a roof that spans further than any wall could hold up. The vocabulary is:

- **The frame and the regular bay.** Columns at a constant spacing set the rhythm of every
  long front in the era, and the rhythm replaces ornament.
- **The cantilever.** Balconies, canopies and upper floors project with nothing beneath them.
  Used once per building at most: it is a statement, and two statements cancel.
- **The thin shell.** A doubly-curved concrete roof landing on a few points, glazed between
  them. The era's one piece of geometric bravado and the assembly hall's whole idea.
- **Ribbon glazing and the spandrel band.** Horizontal layers alternating glass and solid,
  wrapping corners so a building reads as banded rather than as walls with windows in.
- **The plinth.** A low base, a few steps up, the ground held back by a kerb. It is how this
  era gives a modest building dignity without ornamenting it.
- **Flat roofs behind upstands**, so a silhouette ends in a clean horizontal edge, with plant
  and the boiler flue the only things allowed to break it.

**Heights are stated as an order, and this guide deliberately gives no world-unit targets.**
Every stage is contain-fit to its plot by **one uniform scale**, so the same model stands at two
different heights on two different plots and a number is not something the person authoring one
can aim at. The **ranking** is what an era owes, and it survives re-authoring.

**This era's order, tallest first:** the lattice pylon, back out in front by a clear margin
because a lattice is nearly all air; then the slab block, which arrives among the very tall
things and is the biggest single move in the whole dwelling chain; then the heating plant, wide
but tall enough to matter; then the shell-roofed assembly hall, **the first era in which the hall
is not in the top two**; then the test house; then the depot; and the municipal park lowest. **The
era as a whole is a step taller than era seven**, and its real story is that the ranking itself
has changed shape — housing and infrastructure now stand above the civic hall, which is exactly
what the century did. Nothing here reaches the 24-unit ceiling; only era ten's mast is authored to
approach it, and **the ceiling is a cap rather than a target**, because anything taller is
silently squashed.

**Aspect ratio is where this era's extremes live, and it is a property of the models rather than
of the plots.** The pylon should be authored several times taller than it is wide — the most
extreme proportion the run has reached — and because a contain-fit preserves that ratio on any
plot, the proportion is the whole of what makes it read as a tower. The same lever, run the other
way, is what keeps the depot and the park low.

## 4. Ornament and visible technology

**Ornament level: near zero, and the near-zero is itself the statement.** Nothing is applied,
nothing carved, nothing symmetrical for symmetry's sake. What does the decorative work is
already structural: the bay rhythm, the precast panel joint, the shadow line under a canopy,
the change from board-marked to smooth. Where an earlier era put a moulding, this era puts a
tighter joint.

**Lettering is the era's real ornamental language.** Confident sans capitals on a fascia,
generously spaced, in aluminium or painted straight onto concrete. This is the first era whose
buildings are identified by typography rather than by a carved device or a hung sign.

**Visible technology, which the era is proud of:** the cooling tower's steam plume, the pylon and
the substation behind its fence, the roof antenna, the ventilation louvre, the extract cowl, the
weather station, the dial board. Nothing sealed and nothing hidden — burying the machinery is era
nine's move, and era eight would think it dishonest.

**Where D-ERA8 bites hardest here.** Dials are everywhere by right of period, so the line has
to be held: a dial is era ornament on an ordinary building, never an instrument reading that
building's own output. The test house is not the rate-of-change building; it is a workshop that
happens to host FUN.5.

## 5. The seven masses, re-dressed

> **REFERENCE ONLY since 2026-07-29, and read this before drawing from it.** The re-dressing
> ruling (this guide's head banner, and era design §6.1) means **no mass in this section is ever
> re-dressed**. What is still owed here is the era's look for the **six masses** its own **9 new
> nodes** sit on; the treatment of every other mass, and the forward chain through it, is kept as
> the best available description of that mass's **shape language** and will not be built.
>
> **The section is deliberately not cut**, because whoever draws this era's own new building on a
> mass still needs to know what that mass *is* — and because a chain break recorded here is a
> finding about the shapes rather than about a re-dress.

The nine buildings above are new to the era. **These seven are the ones the era inherits** —
era one's six shipped masses plus the Town Hall landmark — and they carry the through-lines.
Per §6.1 of the era design the six ordinary types are dwelling, workshop, store, hall,
tower and yard, and **since 2026-07-29 those six words ARE the mass ids** — so there is one
vocabulary here rather than two. Era one's guide and `buildingAssets.ts` had kept the retired
Gridwork art's own names (*deco*, *slab*, *rowhouse*, *school*, *park*) because that art was
what the ids were coined for; the old-to-new mapping was **already one to one**, so no mass
was reused and no two nodes collapsed onto one id. **Nothing on disk moved** — a GLB filename
is built from the node id, never from the mass. Plus the `town-hall` landmark.

**The plots do not change.** Footprints are frozen for all ten eras (INV-ERA-1) and the table
lives in `../era-01-prehistoric/style-guide.md` §6 rather than here. In words: the hall and the
dwelling share **the square full plot**; the store is **the long two-cell strip**; the yard is
**the two-cell strip on the short axis**; the workshop has **a single tile**; the landmark has
**the wide plot**.

**The tower's plot is deliberately not named**, for the reason
`../era-02-neolithic/style-guide.md` §5 records: it was set to a single tile and reverted to the
square full plot on 2026-07-28, because a contain-fit applies one uniform scale and a smaller plot
therefore shrinks a model whole rather than making it slender. **Slenderness is a property of the
model.** So this guide describes the pylon's proportions and lets era one's table own its cells.

**hall — the assembly hall (the square full plot).**
A single doubly-curved concrete shell landing on four points, glazed between them, so the roof
appears to hover over the corners it does not touch. **The tallest roofed thing in the era, but
for the first time in the run it is not in the top two** — the pylon and the slab block are both
above it. *Through-line: one big communal roof* — and this is where the roof becomes one
continuous surface again for the first time since era one's thatch cone, because the rotunda,
the tholos, the dome and the glazed span were all assembled out of parts and a shell is a single
piece. **Two of the era's new buildings also sit on the hall mass**, FUN.4's monitoring hall and
LF.1's control hall, and neither competes: both are long, low, flat-roofed and ribbon-glazed
against a shell that is tall, centred and curved. Between themselves they separate by plan and
props — LF.1 carries a flat canopy over its entrance, FUN.4 shows its chart room through glass.

**store — the depot (the long two-cell strip).**
A plain concrete-framed shed, roller shutters in a regular rhythm along the long face, a canted
loading apron, **low and long, and the lowest roofed building in the era**. **One bay is left
open**, and that bay is the whole of the chain: *a long horizontal roof, contents on show.*
**Three buildings stand on this mass in this era and they are three separate buildings**, one node
each per D-ERA7: the era-one store re-dressed as the depot, plus LF.3's nuclear weapons facility and
FUN.6's radio workshop from the roster above. **The depot carries the chain and it is the one that
keeps the long open bay** — the other two are blank, nothing visible inside, so one shed you can see
into reads against two you cannot.

**The two roster buildings then had to separate from each other, and the form change has made that
easy where it used to be the hardest pair in the guide.** Their dressings were once written verbatim
alike, down to the canted loading apron, which is two nodes wearing one building and the plainest

## The island (D-ERA26)

The era that **cleans up and reclaims**. Industry's mess is cleared, the shoreline is rebuilt in
concrete, and for the first time in five eras somebody plants something for pleasure.

**The step from era seven, and it is a reversal rather than a continuation.** Every advance so
far has made the island harder, darker or busier. **Era eight makes it pale, clean and empty**,
and that is the read: the soot comes off, the clinker is cleared, the rails are lifted, and the
ground goes from the darkest it has ever been to the palest since era four. **A player advancing
out of era seven should feel the light come back on.** It is the only advance in the run where
the ground gets *lighter*, which is worth protecting — no later era does it again.

**And the ground stops being LAID and starts being POURED**, which is the change underneath the
change in colour. Era seven's floor was a rhythm of identical small units; era eight's is a
**continuous monolithic plane** divided only where it has to be. Unit to monolith is a grain
change of the same size as era seven's composition-to-rhythm, in the opposite direction.

**Ground.** **Poured concrete and asphalt.**

- **Large in-situ concrete bays** with **visible expansion joints** and a **broom finish** — the
  joints are the only lines on the ground and they are structural rather than decorative, so
  they run where the pour had to stop and nowhere else. **They are wide, few and straight**,
  against era seven's thousands of sett joints.
- **Asphalt where vehicles run**, and **painted lines and hatching organising it** — see the
  roads note below.
- **Precisely levelled** landscaped ground **held by low concrete kerbs**, which is how this era
  makes grass: not a slope, a defined flat area with an edge.

**The lifted rails, and this is the era's clearest bit of visible history.** Era seven's track
beds survive as **scars — a stripe of different paving where the track used to be**, filled and
made good but never quite matching. **This section names them, which is what makes them an
authored survival rather than stratified ground** (D-ERA33): era eight is choosing to show where
the rails were, not failing to re-dress that strip.

**This is the last era with soil as a walking surface**, per era ten's closing ledger. From era
nine everything is on structure. Era eight's mown grass and flower beds are the last ground on
this island a person stands on that is actually earth, and it is worth knowing that while
authoring them.

**Shoreline.** A **concrete revetment**: mass-poured, slightly battered, with a rounded coping
and a continuous pipe handrail. **The industrial dock walls are capped and faced over rather than
demolished, so the new edge sits visibly *on* the old one** — another named survival, and the
one that makes the shoreline read as three eras thick. Areas of the old basin are **reclaimed** —
filled and levelled into new flat ground, recognisable because it is **flatter and blanker than
anything around it**, and because its edges are perfectly straight.

**Vegetation.** **Planted, generous and municipal.** Mown grass in broad sweeps, young standard
trees in regular spacing with tree guards, clipped low shrub massing, and flower beds in
geometric blocks. It is the greenest the island has been since the Renaissance and it is
deliberately unlike it: **soft masses rather than clipped geometry**, because this era plants for
amenity rather than for composition. The trees are **young** — planted in this era, not
inherited — and their guards and stakes should say so.

**Where boats land.** Rationalised and quiet. A **concrete wharf** with rubber fenders and cleat
bollards, a small marina of floating pontoons on guide piles, a slipway with a winch. Cargo has
largely moved on — this water is for small craft and pleasure now, which is a real shift and
should read as one.

**Water.** **Cleaned up.** Clear-ish, blue-grey, moving again — the industrial sheen is gone and
the difference is meant to be noticeable. Not the pale see-the-bottom shallow of era one, because
the edge is still a vertical concrete face, but the first water since the Bronze Age that looks
pleasant.

**Signs of habitation at the margins.** Litter bins and painted benches. Streetlights on tapered
concrete columns. A paddling pool. Bicycle stands. A weather station. Signage in confident sans
lettering. Chain-link fence around the power station. **A dial board on a pole**, which is §1's
tally descendant at ground level.

**Against the roads (D-ERA30), and era eight is where the road takes something back from the
ground.** The relation has been *road distinguished from ground* since era four; here the road
is a **different material** from the open ground for the first time since era three — **asphalt
against concrete** — and it also carries the one thing no earlier ground has had: **paint**.
Painted lines, hatching and bay markings are D-ERA30's `markings` course finally becoming
expressible, and the module's own header notes that era one *could not express it at all*. **So
era eight is the era the road stops being a surface and becomes a surface with information on
it.** The island owns the concrete bays, the grass and the kerbs; the road owns the asphalt and
every painted mark on it. **No paint anywhere on the island's own ground** — a hatched bay is a
road, and if the island paints too, the two documents are authoring the same thing again.

**What the era's own absent list rules out of the ground, and one is a live inconsistency this
section fixes.** §2 withholds **composite rainscreen cladding and the sealed jointless skin**
(Information), **emissive surface of any kind** (Cyberpunk), and **exposed conduit and the deck
laid over the ground** (Cyberpunk).

- **NO EMISSIVE SURFACE OF ANY KIND, AND THE STREETLIGHTS ARE THE PROBLEM.** This island authors
  *streetlights on tapered concrete columns*, and a streetlight is exactly where an artist adds a
  glowing head and a pool of light on the concrete. **Era eight may have neither.** A streetlight
  here is an **object** — column, bracket, shade — read by its silhouette, with its lamp face
  taking the ordinary three-tone treatment and emitting nothing. **The ground below it is not
  lit.** This is the same trap era seven has with gas lamps, one era on and with a more tempting
  fitting, and it is flagged here because era eight's absent list is stricter than era nine's:
  era nine permits emissive that is not *"a whole face"*, and **era eight permits none at all.**
- **NO DECK, so nothing here is carried over the ground.** Every surface on this island is on
  soil or on fill. Era nine puts paving on structure and era ten decks over it; **era eight's
  ground rests on the earth and that is the distinction it hands forward.**
- **No sealed jointless surface.** The expansion joints and the broom finish are not texture —
  they are the era **showing that its floor was poured in pieces**, which is exactly what era
  nine's seamless unit paving stops doing.

**Contrast against the buildings, and this is the sharpest instance in the run because the
building and the ground are the SAME MATERIAL AND THE SAME HUE.** Body hue **pale poured concrete
`#B9B5AC`**; the ground is pale poured concrete. Era four had this problem with limestone and era
six with stucco on stone, but in both cases wall and floor were at least *worked differently*.
**Here they are one substance**, and the era's signature makes it worse rather than better:
§1's *thin flat plane* means the most memorable element is a canopy or a slab edge floating clear
of the wall, and a pale thin plane over a pale wall over a pale floor is three pale things in a
vertical line.

**What resolves it:**

1. **The asphalt is the separator, and it is free.** Buildings sit against **dark asphalt** far
   more often than against concrete bays if the layout puts vehicle surface where buildings meet
   the ground. **Use it deliberately rather than incidentally.**
2. **The broad mown grass sweeps.** Large soft green masses are the only non-grey field on this
   island, and they are what stop a pale building being lost in a pale plane. This is a second
   job the planting is doing beyond amenity.
3. **The `#6E9C9A` aluminium-and-teal accent, and the shadow gap under the floating plane.**
   §1's plane reads because it has daylight under it; that dark line is the era's substitute for
   a base, exactly as era six's rusticated plinth was.
4. **Ground goes cooler and slightly darker than the wall.** Same instruction as era four's and
   era six's, and by now it is a pattern rather than three coincidences: **a pale era must
   author its floor below its wall in value, because the horizontal surface takes the top-face
   lightening and will otherwise come out brighter than the thing standing on it.**

**The accumulated city on this ground.** Era seven's soot-dark brick reads superbly against pale
concrete — the two hardest eras in the run rescue each other, exactly as era seven's entry
predicted. **The problem is the pale group**: era four's limestone, era five's lime-wash, era
six's stucco and now era eight's concrete are **four pale eras standing on a pale floor**, and
each is relying on its own dark ground-level element (kerb and coping, oak frame, rusticated
base, shadow gap). **That is now four buildings deep and it is the constraint the hue measurement
pass most needs to know about.**

**The read:** an island being tidied by people who believe the future will be better and that
concrete is how you get there.

## 6. The three construction stages

Every building gets three models. The stage names are fixed in code (`buildingAssets.ts:41`),
and the era's job is to make the sequence read as *this era's* way of building.

| Stage | Engine states | Atomic read |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **`site`** | `not_started`, `preview` | A cleared and levelled rectangle, a site hut, a mixer, and **reinforcement cages laid out flat on the ground** waiting to be stood up. Flat — knee-height at most, and the cages are the tell |
| **`construction`** | `teaching`, `practice` | A **steel tower crane** standing in or beside the plot; **plywood shuttering with strongbacks** on the floor being poured; precast panels stacked on A-frames; the building rising **one floor at a time with the top floor always unfinished** |
| **`built`** | `unfinished`, `complete`, full mastery | The finished building as described in §5. `advanced` (mastery ≥ 2) and full mastery both reuse this model — there is no separate gilded version, and mastery rides above it as stars (`buildingAssets.ts:28-31`) |

**The crane and the identical horizontal slice are what date the era.** A masonry building rises
everywhere at once and unevenly; an iron frame goes up as a skeleton and is then filled in. A
concrete-framed building goes up in identical layers, each a repeat of the one below, with the
top always raw — and a tower crane over it is a silhouette no earlier era could produce.

## The advisor (D-ERA28)

**Oru, dressed and speaking as a systems analyst.** The learner's companion for the whole Atomic
Age, carrying the mini-lesson, the check-your-understanding prompt and the hint exactly as he does
in era one — because he *is* era one's advisor, in this era's clothes and this era's voice.

**Who she is — THE REGISTER, which is per-era and is meant to be.** The person in the control hall
whose job is to know **how fast a thing is changing** and to say so before anyone else notices —
reading trend charts, taking rates off a recorder, sizing what the next hour will look like. This is
the century that measured everything per hour, which is the era design's own reason for putting
rates here, and she is that habit in a person. Not a director and not a technician: she is the one
who explains what the chart means.

**What she is wearing — THE DRESSING, out of this era's four materials and nothing else.** A white
cotton lab coat, unbuttoned, over a plain shift dress in a muted period colour, with low
block-heeled shoes. Horn-rimmed glasses pushed up when she is not reading. A **clipboard** with a
**strip chart** folded over it, the trace visible — this is §1's tally descendant, the dial and the
punched card, in the form a person actually carries. A **slide rule** in the coat's breast pocket,
which is her counterpart to Oru's staff and the era's most honest instrument. An **anodised
aluminium** pen clip is the era's one bright metal note. No lanyard, no badge reel, nothing that
reads as a later century's office.

### The four poses

The same four names as era one. An advance may **shrink** the pose vocabulary (D-ERA28) and every
consumer maps its beat through a total typed table, so keeping the four cannot break a consumer.

| Pose | The read | Where it is used |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **`idle`** | At rest. Clipboard down at her side, glasses up | The resting state; no lesson beat requests it directly |
| **`talk`** | Speaking. The clipboard up, the chart trace turned toward the learner | Every teaching line |
| **`point`** | Tracing along the chart with the slide rule's cursor | A correct answer, a hint, a retry |
| **`squint`** | Glasses down, reading the trace close | Reading the learner's work; authored and available |

**No celebration pose**, following era one; the congratulation is carried by the reward chip and
the mastery star.

### Palette

The systems-analyst dressing sits inside the era palette: lab coat in the off-white that reads
against poured concrete, shift dress in a muted period tone that does not compete with the body hue,
anodised aluminium at the pen. Skin and hair are the only notes outside it.

## Lesson framings

**What this section is.** The era folder carries lesson framing (team lead, 2026-07-29) — how a
lesson sets its mathematics inside the era's world. It replaces the retired island themes spec.

**Three rules it inherits.**

1. **The framing comes from the era's WORLD, not the node's BUILDING** (D-ERA8). The power station
   hosts FUN.10; a lattice tower is not a picture of an absolute-value graph, however tempting
   the V is.
2. **§2's "Explicitly absent" list is a hard boundary.** No composite rainscreen, no sealed
   jointless skin, no emissive surface of any kind, no exposed conduit, and no deck laid over the
   ground. Note also that **brick and timber leave the walls in this era and never come back**,
   so a word problem set among brickwork is one era out of date.
3. **Theming follows the ERA, not the domain.** This era holds five Functions nodes **and all
   four Linear Functions nodes**, from two different domains and two different owners, and the
   whole chapter is framed Atomic.

**The era's context palette — six reusable settings, from this guide's own props:** the **strip
chart** unrolling from a recorder; the **test house** and its instrument racks; the **precinct**
and its planted beds; the **vehicle depot** with its apron and fuel point — *(kept as a world
setting after `LF.3` was re-authored: rule 1 makes a framing a fact about the era's world, and the
era of the car has vehicle aprons whether or not a skill building is one)*; the **pylon line**
marching away across the ground; and the **cooling tower** with steam at its rim.

**One caution specific to this era, and it is a tone one.** The Atomic Age's real history includes
things `Game_Visual_Design_Brief.md` §2's tone bans put out of reach. **Frame this era as the
century that measured and built, never as the century that tested weapons.** Reactors, fallout,
sirens and yield are all off the table; recorders, rates, timetables and power supply are the
era's own material and are plenty.

**This is the rates chapter and the framings should agree with each other across the seam.** Five
`FUN` nodes and four `LF` nodes sit in one chapter under two owners
(`../../../Content_Authoring_Split.md` §10.4 makes the era Gabe's, with Anthony's four `LF` packs
already authored and staying his). The strip chart is the shared object that holds the two halves
together: a `FUN` lesson reads features off it, an `LF` lesson fits a straight line to it.

| Node | The mathematics | Atomic Age framings (2 to 3 per node) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **FUN.4** — read key features from a graph | increasing, decreasing, extrema | (1) **A day's strip chart from the control hall**: where the trace climbs, where it falls, and the highest reading of the shift. (2) **A demand curve over twenty-four hours**, with the morning and evening peaks named off the picture rather than computed. (3) **A test run's temperature trace**, read for when it turned rather than for its value. |
| **FUN.5** — average rate of change | change over an interval | (1) **Units per hour between two chart marks**, which is this century's characteristic sentence. (2) **A vehicle's fuel used between two stops** at the garage apron, as an average over the leg. (3) **The same chart read over two different windows**, giving two different averages, which is the point. |
| **FUN.6** — the difference quotient | the average rate, written generally | (1) **Narrowing the window on the recorder** — the same two chart marks brought closer and closer together, with the arithmetic written once for any gap. (2) **A calibration sheet** that asks for the rate between an arbitrary reading and one a fixed step later. |
| **FUN.9** — transformations; even and odd | shifting, reflecting, stretching a graph | (1) **The same trace, recorded an hour later** — a horizontal shift, on a chart where the shift is literally a distance along the paper. (2) **Turning up the recorder's gain**, which is a vertical stretch and changes nothing about the shape. (3) **A symmetric load pattern** either side of noon, and one that is not, as the even and odd cases. |
| **FUN.10** — absolute-value functions | distance from a target, graphed | (1) **Deviation from a set point**, plotted: the recorder draws a V because it is charting how far off the reading is, either way. (2) **Distance from a substation** along a pylon line, where walking either direction increases it. (3) **A tolerance band on a test sheet**, with the deviation graphed rather than tabulated. |
| **LF.1** — slope as a constant rate of change | what a straight trace means | (1) **A trace that climbs at the same rate all shift** — the chart's own definition of constant, and the contrast with FUN.4's curved one. (2) **A meter reading rising steadily**, so the rate can be taken from any two points. (3) **A flat trace, a rising trace and a falling trace** side by side, classified. |
| **LF.2** — write and graph lines; parallel and perpendicular | the equation of a straight trace | (1) **Two recorders running at the same rate from different starts** — parallel traces. (2) **Laying out a road and its junction on the precinct plan**, where the side road meets it square. (3) **Reading a rate and a starting value off a chart** and writing the line that produced it. |
| **LF.3** — build a linear model | fitting a rule to a description or data | (1) **A standing charge plus a rate per unit**, which is the era's own utility bill. (2) **A garage's fixed callout fee plus a mileage rate**, built from a description into a formula. (3) **Four readings from a test run**, plotted, and the straight line that fits them. |
| **LF.4** — systems of two linear equations | where two lines meet | (1) **Two supplies with different standing charges and rates**, and the usage at which they cost the same — which is the decision the reading is actually for. (2) **Two vehicles leaving at different times at different speeds**, meeting once. (3) **The same pair solved off the chart and then by elimination**, so the picture and the algebra agree. |

## What is authored, and what this guide does not decide

Per §12. Footprints are identical in every era (INV-ERA-1) — see

      descendant.
- [x] **The three construction stages** (`site`, `construction`, `built`) — §6: the tower crane
      and the shutter.
- [x] **The props kit** — §7.

- [x] **The advisor** — above: **Oru dressed and spoken as a systems analyst**, with the four poses,
  the place in the palette and this era's register. *(Re-recorded 2026-07-30 under `D-ET9`, which
  retired the name `Dr Ines Calder`. Still complete: the dressing and the register are what this item
  was ever ticking off.)*
- [x] **Lesson framings** — above, all nine nodes across both halves of the era, with the strip
      chart as the object that holds the `FUN` and `LF` halves together.
- [x] **The era's ROAD design** — closed below.

**Closed on 2026-07-29, and how.**

- **Roads: single surface, markings in the texture.** This era paves in concrete bays with
  painted lines and would want a layered surface to match, and late era plus large island is the
  worst case for `depthPrecision.test.ts`. **Era seven settled the rule for all four late eras**
  — one surface, with kerbs, rails and markings read as material and profile changes within it
  rather than as slabs stacked on it — and this era takes it. Concretely: bay joints and painted
  lines are **in the texture**, the kerb is a profile at the edge of the same surface, and
  nothing is a second coplanar plane. It costs this era nothing that reads at zoom-out.
- **The dwelling's shared yard: an edged, enclosed lawn on the plot, and it is a plan feature.**
  §5 records the break — a deck-access slab block standing free in mown grass has no shared yard
  — and proposes keeping a defined enclosed area of lawn. That is now decided rather than
  proposed, and decided as **plan** rather than landscaping: the block is drawn with a **low wall
  or planted edge enclosing a defined lawn on one face**, which is what the through-line means by
  *several small dwellings round a shared yard* once the dwellings have stacked. It has to be
  decided before the block is modelled, which is exactly why it is decided here.
- **Ownership: this era is GABE'S, entire**, as of 2026-07-29
  (`../../../Content_Authoring_Split.md` §10.4). It was jointly held; the un-split transfers no
  work, because the unauthored half is already Gabe's five `FUN` nodes and Anthony's four `LF`
  packs are authored and stay his.

**Two smaller things, and both are now settled.** The FUN.6 / LF.3 pair's **paper resolution
stands as the decision** (2026-07-29) — a low side-loading radio workshop against a tall end-loading
garage, both blank against the chain's open bay. **A dev-server look is owed as verification rather
than as the decision**, because three concrete sheds on one mass is exactly where a difference that
reads in a description can vanish in the camera; its output is a screenshot or an amendment, not a
question mark. And the **subtitle row this guide owed in place of D-ERA9's naming pass is
DELIVERED**, ratified below — the sentence that asked for it survived the pass that wrote it.
