## 1. The era in one line, and its signature move

**One line:** a trading town that has learned to cast metal, and puts it wherever two
things meet.

**The signature move — metal at the joint.** Bronze arrives in this era, and *where* it
arrives is the whole of the idea: **at the junctions.** Straps round a door post, pins
through a timber lap, hinges, caps over the exposed end of a beam, sheathing on the leaf
of a gate, a collar where a post enters its stone socket. Era one lashed its joints with
cordage and era two hid them inside the thickness of a wall; this era covers them in metal
and then draws the eye to the cover. **The joint is the decoration**, so a building is
read by the small dulled-bright points where its parts are fastened, and there is still no
pattern applied to a surface for its own sake. That is what keeps the motif inside D-ERA8:
metal at a junction is what these people do to everything they build, and it says nothing
about the lesson indoors.

**Why it suits this era specifically.** Trade is the era's premise, and a joint is the
architectural version of a trade — two things brought together that were not made
together. Cast bronze is also the first material on this island that has to be **got**
rather than dug: tin comes from somewhere else, which is why the harbour described below
is the era's real monument. A settlement that has to import half of an alloy is a
settlement with a front door.

**The tally descendant: the stamped weight.** Notches on a post became impressed clay in
era two, and here the mark is struck into metal — a set of graded bronze masses, each with
its value punched into the top face, lying on and beside the balance beam. It is a better
tally than either predecessor, because a weight carries its number *and* its quantity in
the same object. The clay tablet survives beside it as the everyday record; the stamped
weight is the one that has to be trusted, which is why it is always the object nearest the
balance.

## 2. Materials

Four, and the restraint still does most of the period work.

| Material | Read | Notes for modelling |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Mud brick in mass** | The body, in far greater thickness than era two — walls deep enough to corbel over | The same unit and a great deal more of it. The era's massing comes out of wall depth, so keep reveals deep and let a corbelled head read as courses stepped inward, never as a curve cut into a face |
| **Cast bronze** | The era's signature, at joints and fittings only. A warm brown-green patina | Small in area and high in contrast, so it survives zoom-out as glints along an edge rather than as a surface. Dulled and slightly uneven, never polished and never gold |
| **Timber** | Framing, roof structure, gates, hoists, walkways, shear-legs | Squared sections now rather than era one's round poles, and strapped in bronze at every lap. Stay in the muted `#B08968` family so the construction stages read as the same world as the finished buildings |
| **Roughly squared stone, laid dry** | Footings, plinths, thresholds, revetments, and the quay | Squared but undressed, and set without mortar, so the joints stay visibly uneven. This is the era's most important restraint: a mortared ashlar wall here would spend the Classical era's signature four eras early. **Pale and colourless against a body that is neither**: the stone separates from the patina mass on value and on saturation, not on warmth — both are cool now |

**Explicitly absent, because they are later eras' signature moves:** lime mortar and the
true arch, both Classical; fired roof tile, Classical; coursed ashlar, Classical again;
iron, which belongs to the Industrial era; and glass, which waits for the Renaissance.
**What this era stops withholding is cast metal**, which eras one and two both held back
for it — and that is exactly why its first appearance has to be legible at zoom-out rather
than merely true in the description.

**A note on the word "dressed".** The island section below calls the new quay dressed
stone as shorthand for *the best stonework these people can do*. The material is squared
and dry-laid, and era four's island is explicit that the same wall only becomes real
ashlar there, with a moulded coping and mortar joints. Where the two readings meet, this
table wins for the buildings and era four keeps the upgrade.

## 3. Structural language

**Buildings stand up by mass, and mass becomes an expressive tool rather than a fact.**
Era two discovered that a wall can carry its own roof. This era discovers what a *thick*
wall can do that a thin one cannot, and every silhouette in it follows from that.

- **Corbelling.** Courses stepped progressively inward, to close a space overhead or to
  carry an upper storey out beyond the one below. This is the era's structural signature
  and the reason the walls get deep: a corbel is paid for in thickness. It is emphatically
  **not an arch** — the steps must read as steps, and there is no keystone anywhere in
  this era.
- **Mass at scale.** The batter is back, more strongly than in era two, because a wall
  this thick is built with a base wider than its head. It reads as weight, which is the
  era's whole affect.
- **Timber where something has to be spanned.** A squared beam on a row of posts, capped
  and strapped in bronze at every lap, with the metal marking exactly where the load
  transfers.
- **An upper storey, and an external walkway.** The first level in the run that people
  stand on above the ground — a timber gallery along one side of a courtyard, a walkway
  carried across the head of a tower. Where a walkway crosses, its bearers project through
  the wall and are left showing.
- **Openings still small and still lintelled**, but more of them and much deeper, because
  the reveal is now as thick as an arm's length. A deep reveal is free shadow and it does
  more for the silhouette than another shape would.
- **Roofs by plan.** Timber-and-reed cones over the drums, low ridges over the rectangles,
  awnings on posts over working ground, and the era's one refinement: a **shingled timber
  canopy**, which is a tiled roof made of split wood. Fired roof tile is Classical and
  must not appear here.
- **Ground metalled rather than paved.** Rammed gravel and stone chips with kerbstones, so
  the routes read as constructed while the surface stays granular — fitted slabs belong to
  era four.

**Heights are stated as an order, and this guide deliberately gives no world-unit targets.**
Every stage is contain-fit to its plot by **one uniform scale**, so the same model stands at
two different heights on two different plots and a number is not something the person
authoring one can aim at. The **ranking** is what an era owes, and it survives re-authoring.

**This era's order, tallest first:** the gate tower, standing clear of everything else; then
the assembly rotunda; then the furnace court, wide rather than tall but now genuinely
massive; then the bonded store and the courtyard house, level with one another; then the
casting workshop; and the weighing yard lowest. **The era as a whole is a step taller than
era two**, and its climb is mostly in mass rather than in height — a thick wall reads heavy
before it reads tall. Nothing here comes close to the 24-unit ceiling; only era ten's mast
is authored to approach it. **The weighing yard's ground plane never rises in any of the ten
eras**, even though its awning and balance beam stand well above it, and that is the yard
chain's entire contribution to the run.

## 4. Ornament and visible technology

**Ornament level: low, and it is the fitting.** Everything decorative is a piece of
hardware doing a job — a strap, a boss, a stud, a cap on a beam end, a collar at a post
foot, a hinge left large enough to see. Beside it the era can honestly support two flat
devices: **the corbel course**, which is ornament and structure in the same move, and a
small amount of impressed work in brick, such as a chevron or a rope moulding under an
eaves line. Nothing carved, nothing painted, no figure work.

**Visible technology, and this is the richest era so far:** the smelting hearth with its
bellows, the crucible and the two-piece mould, the charcoal heap, the balance beam and its
stamped weights, lashed shear-legs with a rope and a counterweighted basket, the barred
gate, and **the first wheeled cart on the island** — era two took the wheel as rotation
only, and this is the era that puts it under a load. The potter's wheel and the quern
carry over.

**Nothing that implies iron, mortar or the arch.** No forged edge, no rivet in ferrous
grey, no mortar joint, no voussoir. The Classical era needs mortar and the arch to arrive
together, and the Industrial era needs iron to arrive as a shock.

## The island (D-ERA26)

**This is the era the island stops being landscape and becomes site.** The single most important
change in the whole ten-era arc happens here, and it happens at the water's edge.

**The step from era two, stated first because that is what a player sees at the advance.** Era
two broke the ground by hand and left everything else alone: small crooked plots, boundaries
that were heaps of what the plot gave up, and a shore with nothing built on it. **Era three
brings TRACTION and STONE, and each transforms a different half of the island.** Traction
regularises the fields — an animal that can pull a cart can pull an ard, so the furrow runs the
full length of a plot for the first time and the plots grow to match. Stone builds the edge. **A
player advancing out of era two should read two things at once: the fields have gone straight,
and the water has grown a wall.**

**The first real quay and retaining wall.** Squared stone, laid dry in courses, meeting the water
at a **straight line** for the first time in the island's history. Where every earlier era
shelved into gravel, there is now an edge you could stand a cart on: a rubble-cored retaining
wall faced with squared blocks, a level stone apron behind it, and a short **quay** running out
into the shallows with a rubble mole sheltering the water beside it. The wall does not go all
the way round — perhaps a third of the shoreline. **The contrast is the point:** built stone on
the harbour side, era one's raw gravel beach still visible round the back.

**Ground.** Three surfaces now, where era two had worked soil and untouched tussock.

- **Terraced plots** where the ground slopes, held by **low dry-laid stone revetments** — the
  first time soil has been moved and then held. A terrace reads as a **step in the ground with a
  stone lip**, and the lip is the thing to make legible: it is the same squared-and-dry-laid
  stone as the quay, at knee height, and it is what says the fields and the harbour were built
  by the same people.
- **Metalled routes** between them: rammed gravel and stone chips, **edged with kerbstones**,
  running as a network rather than as worn lines. See the roads note below — this surface is
  D-ERA30's and the island states only its relation to it.
- **Level bare earth and grass** between the terraces, still unpaved, but level in a way it was
  not: somebody has moved soil on purpose and stopped.

**The furrows change, and this is the ground's clearest single tell against era two.** Era two's
were *short, shallow, crooked and interrupted* because they were hoed by hand. **Here they are
long, straight and parallel, running the full length of a plot**, because a wooden ard drawn by
an animal does not stop for a stone — it goes round the field, not round the obstacle. A
terraced plot of full-length parallel furrows beside era two's crooked patches is a difference
anybody reads without knowing why.

**Field boundaries.** The clearance heaps of era two have been **rebuilt as proper walls** —
still dry-laid, because there is no mortar in this era, but **coursed, faced, straight-ish and
waist-high**, with the stone visibly sorted rather than tipped. Gateways have **stone posts**.
The plots themselves are larger and more regular, and **for the first time a few of them are the
same shape as each other** — which is the fields catching the discipline the quay brought.

**Vegetation.** Cultivation is confident: **larger fields in recognisable blocks**, a small
orchard or two in rough rows, and the first **planted** trees — set where somebody wanted shade,
not where seed happened to land. **That is this era's own first and era two is forbidden it**, so
it should be unmistakable: a short line of the same species at the same spacing, which is a thing
no earlier island has anywhere on it. Wild scrub is pushed back to the steep ground and the far
margin. Reeds survive only where the new wall has not reached.

**Where boats land.** Properly, now. Plank-decked timber staging on stone footings alongside
the quay, **stone mooring blocks** with rope-worn grooves, a crane frame of lashed timber for
lifting cargo, and beached working boats with sails furled. This is a harbour rather than a
landing place.

**Water.** Still calm and pale, but the mole has made the harbour side **flat and dead** where
the open side still moves. Silt has begun to collect inside the mole, going pale brown and
shallow. **This is the first era in which the water has two states**, and the boundary between
them is a straight line drawn by a structure — which is worth more than the mole itself.

**Signs of habitation at the margins.** Stacked ingots and ore under a lean-to near the quay.
Charcoal heaps and a smelting hearth with **slag spilling downhill from it — the first genuinely
*industrial* scar on the ground**, and the ancestor of era seven's clinker. Amphora-like storage
jars in rows. A scatter of bronze offcuts where the metalworkers sit. **Cart ruts**, which no
earlier island has, because the wheeled cart is this era's authored first.

**Against the roads (D-ERA30).** The metalled route is the **road**, and it has no authored
surfacing row yet — `ERA_ROAD_SURFACING` covers eras one and two only, so era three currently
falls back to one bare-earth course. **Whoever writes that row owns the material; this section
owns only the relation**, which is: era three is the first era in which a road is **built up
above the ground rather than worn down into it**. Era one's and era two's treads are *sunken and
darker*; a metalled route is **raised, drier, paler and kerbed**. That inversion is the advance,
and it should survive the surfacing row being written.

**What the era's own absent list rules out of the ground.** §2 withholds **lime mortar**,
**coursed ashlar**, **fired roof tile**, **iron** and **glass**, and each of the first three is
something a stone harbour reaches for by reflex:

- **No mortar anywhere, including underwater.** Every wall on this island — quay, revetment,
  terrace lip, field wall — is **laid dry**. That is the constraint doing the most work here,
  because it caps how straight and how tall any of them can be, and it is what keeps era four's
  ashlar-in-lime a genuine arrival.
- **No dressed or finely coursed stone.** §2's material is *roughly squared stone, laid dry*, so
  faces are near-flat rather than flat, joints are visible and uneven, and no block is rubbed.
  **The quay is impressive by being big, never by being fine.**
- **No iron.** No iron tool marks on the stone, no iron cramp, no iron ring or chain at a
  mooring. Mooring is **stone blocks with rope-worn grooves**, which is why they are authored
  that way rather than as bollards.
- **No metal ploughshare.** §2 confines cast bronze to **joints and fittings**, and a share
  dragged through grit is neither. The ard is **all wood**, which is exactly why the furrows can
  be long and straight and still shallow.
- **No fired tile or paving.** Nothing fired is laid as a surface until the Classical era.

**Contrast against the buildings.** Body hue **bronze patina `#5F6D4E`**, accent **burnished
bronze `#A97740`** — and the ground it stands on is grey dry-laid stone, pale metalled gravel and
bare earth. **This era is comfortable**, and it is worth saying so — but say it for the right
reason, because the 2026-07-31 body changed the mechanism and not just the number. **A patina
mass and cool grey stone are both cool, so nothing here is separated by warmth any more.** What
separates them is **value first and saturation second**, and both are properties of `#5F6D4E`
rather than of its neighbours:

- **Value.** At **L\* 44.1** the patina is **the darkest body in the first six eras**, and not
  narrowly: era one's terracotta is the next darkest at **L\* 62.8**, eighteen points above it.
  So the mass reads as a dark shape on pale ground — grey footings, paler metalled gravel, and
  §1.1's parchment — and it does so **on all three of its faces**. §2 of the Art Bible lightens
  the top toward white by 0.26 and darkens the right toward black by 0.28, and both are
  **monotonic** blends, so the darker of two materials is still the darker one on every face it
  turns. A separation that lives in value therefore does not depend on which way a wall faces,
  which is exactly what the old warm-against-cool reading could not promise.
- **Saturation.** The stone is **effectively colourless** and the body is not. At **C\* 19.3**
  the wall holds a definite green that no grey footing, kerb or terrace lip competes with, while
  staying well inside era one's saturation ceiling of C\* 38.3.

**The figure-ground reading comes out stronger than before rather than merely different**:
**ΔE76 48.43 from §1.1's parchment ground, still the largest of any body in the first six eras**
(era one's is 41.52, era five's 26.88, era four's 12.37). *(It was 50.85 under the ochre body; the
value moved by 2.4 and the ranking did not.)* **Warm-against-cool survives, but it has moved
inboard** — it is now the relation between the **burnished accent and the wall it is fastened to**
(`#A97740` at h 70.0° against the body's 126.1°, ΔE76 34.7), which is §4's point that the fitting
is the brighter note, and it is a claim about the building rather than about the ground.

The risk is the other way — **the terrace lips and field walls are the same
grey stone as the quay and the building footings**, so keep the terrace lips low and unfaced so
they never compete with the harbour wall for the eye. Era one's terracotta and era two's pale
straw-buff brick still stand here and both read fine against grey.

**The read:** a trading settlement that has learned to cut stone and immediately used it on the
water, because the water is where the wealth arrives. The island now has a front door.

## 6. The three construction stages

All sixteen buildings get three models each, which is where this era's cumulative cost is
really paid. The stage names are fixed in code (`buildingAssets.ts:41`), and the era's job
is to make the sequence read as *this era's* way of building.

| Stage | Engine states | Bronze Age read |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **`site`** | `not_started`, `preview` | The trench set out with **pegged string lines and a plumb-bob hanging on a cord**, and stone footings sorted into heaps by size beside it. Flat, ankle-height at most, but unmistakably *measured* — this is the first era whose site stage shows an intention rather than a clearing |
| **`construction`** | `teaching`, `practice` | **Lashed timber shear-legs with a rope and a counterweighted basket**, lifting stone and brick, with the courses rising behind it and a bronze-fitted door leaf waiting on trestles at the side. The hoist is the tallest thing on the plot while the building is going up |
| **`built`** | `unfinished`, `complete`, full mastery | The finished building as described in §5 and in the roster. `advanced` (mastery ≥ 2) and full mastery both reuse this model — there is no separate gilded version, and mastery rides above it as stars (`buildingAssets.ts:28-31`) |

**The hoist is the era's dating device, and the string lines are the underrated half of
it.** A player who watches a building rise ten times over the run should be able to name
the era from the scaffold alone: era one is stakes and rope, era two is an earth ramp and
a drying floor of green bricks, and this era is the first with a **machine** on the plot.
Shear-legs are three poles and a rope, so they cost almost nothing to describe and they
change the reading of the whole stage.

**Keep the plumb-bob.** Era two built off a ramp by eye and by course; setting out with
string and a weighted cord is the moment building becomes measurement on this island, and
it is the visual prerequisite for era four's full-size setting-out drawing.

## Lesson framings

**What this section is.** The era folder carries lesson framing (team lead, 2026-07-29) — how a
lesson sets its mathematics inside the era's world. It replaces the retired island themes spec.

**Three rules it inherits.**

1. **The framing comes from the era's WORLD, not the node's BUILDING** (D-ERA8). The foundry
   hosts LIN.3; it does not illustrate rearranging a formula.
2. **§2's "Explicitly absent" list is a hard boundary.** No lime mortar, no true arch, no fired
   roof tile, no coursed ashlar, **no iron**, and **no glass**. A word problem may not contain
   them, and it may not contain a gauge, a sensor, a dial or a reading taken by an instrument —
   this era measures by **balancing one thing against another**, which is the whole point of it.
3. **Theming follows the ERA, not the domain.** This era is one whole domain, so nothing splits.

**The era's context palette — six reusable settings, from this guide's own props:** the **hand
balance** and its graded weight set; the **smelting hearth** and its bellows; the **sealed bonded
store** with its one lockable door; the **quay** and the boats that tie up at it; the **ingot** as
a unit that can be split or stacked; and the **town wall** with its bastion and its safe distance.

> **THE INGOTS IN THIS FILE KEEP THE WORD, deliberately — all seven of them.** The era's *material
> id* was renamed `ingots` → `bronze` on 2026-07-29 (`civic-buildings.md` §1), and every occurrence
> in this guide is **a physical object in the world or in a word problem**, not the resource: the
> ingot-weighing yard, the ingots visible in the bonded store, the stacked ingots under a lean-to
> near the quay, the casting props, the ingot as a unit above, and `LIN.1`'s cargo of *"a counted
> number of standard ingots plus an odd remainder"* on the balance beam.
>
> **Which is the rename's own logic rather than an exception to it.** Bronze is the substance; an
> ingot is the shape it is cast in, and the shape is precisely what makes it splittable and
> stackable on a balance — the thing `LIN.1` needs. **A find-and-replace over this folder would
> break the era's best framing device**, so the stockpile is bronze and the objects stay ingots.

**The balance is this era's single best framing device and it is worth saying why.** A linear
equation is two things that must come out level, and this era owns the only instrument in the
whole ten-era run that shows that physically. Use it for the equation nodes without apology.
**What it cannot do is inequalities** — a balance that tips tells you *which* is heavier and not
by how much — and that limit is useful rather than awkward: it is exactly the step from LIN.1 to
LIN.4, and an author should let the balance fail at the right moment rather than stretching it.

| Node | The mathematics | Bronze Age framings (2 to 3 per node) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **LIN.1** — solve one-variable linear equations | isolate the unknown, keeping both sides equal | (1) **What weight of tin balances this copper** — the era design's own one-line reason for placing linear equations here, and the most literal equation in the game. (2) **Take the same amount off both pans**, which is what "do the same to both sides" *is* before it is a rule. (3) **A cargo of known total** made of a counted number of standard ingots plus an odd remainder. |
| **LIN.3** — rearrange a formula to isolate a chosen variable | the same relation, solved for something else | (1) **One alloy recipe read three ways** at the foundry — how much tin for this copper, how much copper for this tin, how much of both for a finished weight. (2) **A charge, a yield and a loss**, where the smelter needs whichever of the three he does not already know. (3) **A toll reckoned per boat and per load**, rearranged when the quay wants the load from the toll. |
| **LIN.4** — linear and compound inequalities; intervals | a range rather than a point | (1) **The bonded store's terms:** a consignment is accepted only if it weighs at least the declared minimum and no more than the seal allows — two conditions at once, which is a compound inequality and also a real door policy. (2) **Draught and the quay** — a boat may tie up only between two water depths. (3) **A firing window**: the hearth works above one heat and spoils above another. |
| **LIN.5** — absolute-value equations | distance from a mark, either side | (1) **A weight certified to within so much of the standard** — the official's whole job, and the error may fall either way. (2) **Two positions the same distance along the wall** from a named gate, in opposite directions. (3) **A mould that has shrunk or swollen** off its nominal size by a stated amount. |
| **LIN.6** — absolute-value inequalities | a tolerance band | (1) **The tolerance a weight must hold** to keep its stamp: within so much of standard, or it is rejected — the inequality version of LIN.5's equation, in the same yard, which is the cleanest pairing in this era. (2) **A safe distance from the wall**, stated as no nearer than and no further than a mark. (3) **A batch's permitted variation** before the whole cast is broken up and re-melted. |

  beside the locked bays** — is adopted. It keeps the through-line legible (there is still

  and it is the same technique the whole folder uses for a chain break: bend it with a plan
  feature rather than abandon it. Recorded as a genuine **strain** rather than a clean
  inheritance, so era four knows what it is inheriting.
- **Footprints for the five new nodes are decided**, since INV-ERA-1 fixes them forever.
  **LIN.4 (store) takes the long plot; LIN.1 (hall), LIN.3 (workshop), LIN.5 (yard) and LIN.6
  (tower) take the square full plot.** The bonded store is a run of bays behind a counter, which
  is the long plot's own shape and is what makes the open bay beside the locked ones legible. The
  weighing yard stays square because it is a working ground you stand in the middle of, and the
  bastion stays square because era one measured that a smaller plot makes a tower shorter rather
  than more slender.

  gravel beach still visible round the back. A third is enough for the quay to read as *the front
  of the town* at zoom-out while leaving the original island legible underneath, which is the arc
  `../README.md` describes — the Bronze Age is the moment the island stops being landscape and
  becomes site, and that moment is spoiled if the whole shore hardens at once.
