Ten folders, one per era in `../2026-07-27-era-progression-design.md` §3. Each holds
**exactly two documents**, split along **D-ERA7**'s two classes of building:

- **`style-guide.md`** — the **SKILL buildings**: the ones *earned* 1:1 with lessons, plus
  the Town Hall landmark. Materials, structural language, roof and support forms, palette,
  ornament, visible technology, and the era's signature move — **and the ISLAND** it all stands
  on (**D-ERA26**): ground surface, shoreline and water edge, vegetation, how boats meet the
  land, and the signs of habitation at the margins — **and the ADVISOR** the learner talks to
  (**D-ERA28**): who they are, what they wear, their named poses, and how they sit in the era
  palette.
- **`civic-buildings.md`** — the **CIVIC buildings**: the ones the player *buys*, which
  carry the city's **food, livability and happiness**. About four era-exclusive types per
  era, each frozen in the era it was built.

**D-ERA20** authors ten of each; **D-ERA21** keeps the art itself outside this repo, so
these documents are the deliverable — not models and not a kit spec.

**Image inspiration** goes straight into the era folder alongside them. Reference it from
either document with a plain relative path, for example
`![a battered rammed-earth wall](rammed-earth.jpg)`.

**To turn an era's two documents into a brief an artist can generate from**, invoke the
`era-building-briefs` project skill (`/era-building-briefs`, and see
`.cursor/skills/era-building-briefs/SKILL.md`). It emits a short theme brief that fixes the
era's materials, palette, structural vocabulary and signature move, then one description per
building — the era's own skill buildings, its hub and its four civic types — with every name
read from these documents at run time rather than from a list. **Output goes to
`output/imagegen/`, not into the era folder**, which holds exactly two documents. Era two's is
at `output/imagegen/era-02-neolithic-building-briefs/`.

**Why the split.** Skill buildings are **earned** 1:1 with lessons; civic buildings are
**bought** and **provide** the capacities. Both are now **frozen** in the era they were built,
so the split is about **how you get a building**, not about what it does or how often it is
redrawn.

## Three things the ten guides do jointly, which no single guide can carry

Reading one guide will not show these, so they are recorded here.

**Absences are as load-bearing as presences.** Era one withholds metal, fired brick and mortar
precisely so that eras three, two and four can have them as signature moves, and every guide states
what it does not use yet and which later era owns it. That accounting is what makes the ten read as
one sequence rather than as ten independent looks. **Era ten is the only guide whose absence
paragraph looks backwards**, since nothing follows it to withhold for: it closes the arc by listing
what has left the island for good and which era last used it.

> **SCOPE NOTE, 2026-07-29.** The three joint properties below were written for a city where
> every building re-dressed at every advance. **The first and third are unaffected; the second —
> the seven forward chains — is now REFERENCE rather than a deliverable**, because no mass is
> ever re-dressed. It is kept in full: a chain is the clearest available statement of what a mass
> *is* as a shape, and whoever draws a later era's own new node on that mass still needs it.
> The **fourth** (construction stages) is unaffected — a building is still raised on screen once,
> in its own era.

**Seven through-lines chain forward, and where they break the guides say so.** Era one sets them —
one big communal roof, a long horizontal roof with contents on show, several small dwellings round a
shared yard, a shaded open floor with something to mark on, open ground deliberately kept open, a
slender open frame you climb, a sheltered communal fire — and each later guide re-dresses all seven
so the mass is plausibly the same building matured. **Three genuine breaks are recorded rather than
smoothed over:** the tower closes into a solid drum in era two, the store's contents stop being
visible in era ten, and the workshop's open floor cannot survive era nine's sealed laboratory at all.
Each names Evan as owner.

**An inherited building and a roster node that share a dressing are TWO buildings, and the guides
now say so explicitly wherever it comes up.** This is the one thing three separate authors got wrong
in the same way, so it is recorded here rather than left to be re-derived. From era four onward most
eras put a new node on a mass one of era one's seven already occupies, and because a mass has one
architectural vocabulary per era the two end up described almost identically — era eight's depot and
parts store, era ten's data store, era nine's residential block. It is tempting to write "this is
`LF.3`'s building" and be done. But **D-ERA7 is one node, one lesson, one building**, and INV-ERA-1
gives era one's seven their own frozen cells, so they are separate buildings that happen to be
wearing the same clothes. Each guide therefore names the roster instance as separate, says **which
instance carries the through-line** (always the inherited one), and separates the pair by scale,
plan or props rather than by geometry — era eight's is the clearest, one shed you can see into
against two you cannot.

> ## ⚑ 2026-07-30 — THE LADDER SETS WHAT A CITY COSTS. IT NO LONGER SETS WHAT THE FOUR BUILDINGS COST

> **THE RULING (team lead, verbatim):** *"the buildings shouldn't all have the same price ratios and be
> priced the exact same relative to each other across eras. Doesn't need to be drastic; just some kind
> of variance would be nice."*

> the prices, so the catalogue read as **one price shape doubled nine times**. At era-one scale the
> forty costs collapsed into five vectors: eras one, two and three all ran `80 / 80 / 100 / 120`; eras
> five, six and nine all ran `80 / 90 / 100 / 120`; eras four and eight `80 / 100 / 100 / 120`; and
> only eras seven and ten had a shape of their own. **Era two was the worst case** — era one's four

> varies is **which of the four an era makes dearest, and by how much**.

## What each guide owes

Per §12: **materials, structural language, roof and support forms, palette, level of
ornament, visible technology, and the era's signature move.**

Plus, per **D-ERA26** (§5.2), **the island itself** — its terrain, shoreline, water treatment and
vegetation are per-era art, re-dressed at every advance. *(This read "alongside the buildings";
since the 2026-07-29 ruling the buildings do not re-dress and **D-ERA26 is untouched** — the
island, the roads, the advisor and the Town Hall are the four surfaces that still do.)* **All ten island
descriptions are authored**, and they follow one arc: era one is a shoreline camp on
barely-altered land; **era two works the land — fields, furrows, boundaries, penned animals and
stored grain, which is that era's whole identity and not a detail of it**; the **Bronze Age brings the first
quay and retaining wall, which is the moment the island stops being landscape and becomes site**;
and from there it hardens through paved ground, engineered banks and canalised water until the
shoreline is wholly constructed and the original island is only readable underneath.

Note that **§5 governs how much land there is and D-ERA26 governs what it looks like**, and the
two are coupled: `ISLAND_GRASS_RADIUS` and `SEA_RADIUS` do not follow the buildable radius, so
every era that grows the island also re-authors its shore.

### THE ISLAND CARRIES THE ERA; THE BUILDINGS CARRY THE LESSON — D-ERA32

**Ruled 2026-07-30 by the team lead, recorded as D-ERA32** at
`../2026-07-27-era-progression-design.md` §5.2a; **D-ERA33** beside it settles whether the
island re-dresses wholesale. The rule had been implicit since D-ERA26 and was never written
down, which is why every guide's island section was its least specific part and why **beat four
of the era-advance ceremony was its thinnest beat** (`../2026-07-29-era-trial-contract.md`
§19.1, D-ET8 — *"the island re-themes — ground, water, vegetation and roads"*, with nothing
anywhere saying what that consists of).

**All ten island sections were authored to artist depth on 2026-07-30 on the strength of it**,
and this section carries the anatomy, the ladder and the two cross-era constraints that no
single guide can see. **Era one is the exception and is deliberately not re-authored** — it is
the floor the other nine depart from, its art is generated and its ground is shipped, and its
own §6b records why in full.

**The principle, in one line: the island carries the era, and the buildings carry the lesson.**

**Why each half is the way round it is.** A skill or civic building freezes because it is **a
record of what the learner built and when** — it is theirs, and re-dressing it edits their
history. The island re-dresses because it is **the age you are standing in**, and an age is a
fact about the present rather than about what anybody has done. That is not a new decision: it is
the reason the four surfaces still moving under D-ERA26 / D-ERA28 / D-ERA30 and the re-dressing
ruling — **the island, the roads, the advisor and the Town Hall** — are exactly the four that
describe the present, and the reason the frozen ones are exactly the ones a learner earned. The
head banner derives the same split from art economics (*per-era art freezes where it
multiplies*); **this is the same line drawn from meaning instead of from cost, and the two agree,
which is worth more than either alone.**

**The consequence is the load-bearing half.** An era's identity has to be visible **in the
ground**, because the ground is the only large surface that changes at an advance. If an era's
distinctness lives in its architecture, the learner who advances sees the city they already had
with one new building in it, and the advance reads flat however good that building is. **If it
lives in the land, the whole screen changes at once.**

**Era two is the case this was ruled on.** Its guide had made its identity *sun-dried mud brick
with square corners for the first time in the run* — which is an architect's distinction between
two brown settlements, and a player sees brown huts and then slightly straighter brown huts. **The
Neolithic Revolution is agriculture, not masonry**, and era two's own resource is already
**grain**, its buildings already a Granary, a Seed House and a Sheep Pen. The farming was in the
content and was not the headline. It is the headline now
(`era-02-neolithic/style-guide.md` §1 and its island section). **The brick is unchanged and still
true; it is the BUILDING language rather than the era's signature move.**

#### What an island re-theme consists of

**Nobody had said, which is why beat four had nothing to animate.** All ten island sections
already share one anatomy — arrived at independently by four authors, never written down. It is
stated here so the next one can be authored to it rather than re-derive it, and so beat four has
a list of surfaces to change.

**Six axes and a read**, measured off the ten sections at `e3b715a` rather than asserted:

| Axis | What it answers | Era two's answer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Ground** | what the walking surface is MADE OF, and what has been done to it | worked soil raked dark against pale untouched tussock — a settlement's worth of plots, in four states at once |
| **Vegetation** | what grows, whether it was planted, and how much of it is placed | sown cereal in lumpy hand-sown patches against wild scrub, and the separation IS the era |
| **Shoreline** | how the land meets the water, and whether the meeting is built | unbuilt, with the first intentional touch |
| **Where boats land** | what arriving looks like | a gravel slipway worn broad, with a timber skid track |
| **Water** | its colour, depth, cleanliness, and whether it is worked | pale and shallow, with fish traps set in it |
| **Signs of habitation at the margins** | the props that date the settlement away from its buildings | grain on drying racks, a quern, storage pits, a midden with potsherds |
| **The read** | one sentence a modeller can check the whole island against | *a settlement that has started to farm and has not yet started to build* |

**Two documented departures from the six, both correct.** Era one folds **Water** into its opening
paragraph instead of giving it a heading, because era one's water is the baseline and has nothing
to depart from; era three names its **Shoreline** *"The first real quay and retaining wall"*,
because that is the era's headline and burying it under a generic heading would hide it. **And the
two farming eras carry a SEVENTH axis, `Field boundaries`** — eras two and three only, since they
are the only eras in which the land is divided rather than paved.

**The ground is the axis that must carry the age**, and the other five support it. It is the
largest surface, it is under everything else, and it is the one a player cannot look away from.

**Each of the nine authored sections also closes with three notes the folder needs and no single
guide used to carry**: its **relation to the roads**, its **absent-list check on the ground**,
and its **contrast against the buildings**. The two cross-era rules those produced are below.

#### The roads are the eighth axis, and ground-versus-road was ONE DECISION MADE TWICE

**Found while authoring, and it reached all ten sections.** D-ERA30 gives the road surface to
`ERA_ROAD_SURFACING` in `apps/web/src/city/roadSurfacing.ts` — authored for eras one and two,
absent for the other eight, with a bare-earth fallback. **But every island section's `Ground`
paragraph was also authoring the road**: era two's opened on *"hollow ways"* while
`ERA_TWO_HOLLOW` is the road's tread course; era three's names *metalled routes edged with
kerbstones*; era four's *"the main routes"*; era seven's *"setts and engineering brick where
carts and rails run"*; era eight's *"asphalt where vehicles run, painted lines"*. **Two documents
were specifying one surface, in ten places, with no rule saying which wins.**

**The rule, applied in all nine authored sections: the island owns the ground BETWEEN the roads
and states its RELATION to them; D-ERA30 owns the road tile itself.** The relation is a **value
and construction** statement — sunken or raised, darker or paler, same material or different —
never a second specification of the material. **D-ERA30's era-one entry already worked exactly
this way** and is the model: the verge is the island's ambient worn-earth tone *exactly*, the
tread is darker than every worn tone the island uses, and *"that two-sided value contrast is also
what makes the path read as SUNKEN rather than laid on top."*

**The relation is itself a ladder, and it inverts four times**, which no one guide could see:

| Era | The road against the ground |
| --- | --- |
| **1-2** | **Sunken and darker.** Worn down into the ground, no edge |
| **3** | **Raised, drier, paler, kerbed.** The first road built UP rather than worn down |
| **4** | **The same stone as the ground**, separated by kerb, gutter and wear polish |
| **5** | **A state of the ground**, not a thing laid on it — ruts, patches and standing water concentrate along it, gutter down the middle, no kerb |
| **6** | **A member of the ground's pattern** — a band the composition acknowledges, same stone |
| **7** | **The only hard regular surface on the island**, reading harder and paler than the clinker waste around it |
| **8** | **A different material again** — asphalt against concrete — and the first ground anywhere carrying **paint** |
| **9** | **The same material, a different module** — flush, kerbless, separated by tactile and colour rather than by height |
| **10** | **The ground plus LIGHT** — lit seams and emissive banding let into a level deck |

**Eight surfacing rows are still owed** and each guide now says what its row must express, so
whoever writes them has a target rather than a blank. **If a future row contradicts its island
section, one of the two is wrong and they should be reconciled rather than left to disagree** —
era four's and era six's say so explicitly, because those are the two most at risk.

#### The ten-era ladder, one line each

**Each section is now written explicitly against its predecessor**, because a step the player can
see at the boundary is the whole point. Read down the third column to see the run.

| Era | The ground | The step from the era before |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1 Prehistoric** | Packed bare earth and dry tussock, worn to soil where people walk. Nothing planted, in rows or fenced | **The floor.** Barely-altered land, and the baseline every later shore departs from |
| **2 Neolithic** | Worked soil in a settlement's worth of small hand-broken plots, in four states at once — freshly broken, standing crop, stubble, fallow — against untouched tussock | **Wild becomes WORKED.** The ground is done something to for the first time: turf stripped, stones picked out, furrows hoed, a boundary put round it |
| **3 Bronze Age** | Terraced plots behind dry-laid stone revetments, metalled routes, level bare earth between | **Traction and stone.** An animal that can pull a cart can pull an ard, so furrows run a plot's full length and the fields go straight — and the water grows a wall |
| **4 Classical** | Fitted stone slabs to a camber, kerbed and guttered, laid to a regular bond that runs under everything | **Loose aggregate becomes a FLOOR.** One continuous plane that does not change where a building stops — and the fields leave the island |
| **5 Medieval** | Era four's floor sunk, tilted, rutted and patched; new ground cobbled in rough fans; beaten earth and a gutter down the lane | **STATE, not material.** The one era that brings almost no new surface: the floor that was to a line is no longer to a line, and the camber is gone |
| **6 Early Modern** | Geometric paving — banded, diagonal or radiating — composed across the whole open ground, on stepped platforms with moulded edges | **PATTERN and level.** The same stone re-laid to a drawing, with the setting-out cut into it: bench marks and a meridian line |
| **7 Industrial** | Setts and engineering brick with **iron rails let into them**, cinder and clinker elsewhere, soot-darkened unevenly and downwind | **The light goes out.** Clean pale composition becomes dark identical machine-made units organised by where things must be dragged |
| **8 Atomic** | Poured concrete bays with wide expansion joints and a broom finish, asphalt, painted lines; the rails lifted, their beds surviving as scars | **The only advance that gets LIGHTER**, and the ground stops being laid and starts being POURED. The last era with soil underfoot |
| **9 Information** | Precise unit paving on structure — large-format slabs, banded setts, resin-bound gravel — with flush covers, tactile paving and uplighters in the pattern | **Poured becomes LAID.** A monolith divided by a few structural joints becomes thousands of small units — the same unitising its buildings do |
| **10 Cyberpunk** | A **deck** of perforated metal, dark composite and resin over the older ground, with gaps and grilles you see down through. Wet, reflective, and nothing is soil | **Sealed becomes OPEN.** Era nine's floor hid what was under it; era ten lets you look through to it, which closes the arc that began on bare earth |

#### The principle checked against all ten eras, 2026-07-30

**No era genuinely cannot carry its age in the ground, so the principle holds in all ten and
nothing breaks it.** That is the result. The qualifications are the useful part.

**Three eras carry it by something other than a new material, and each mode is a legitimate
second answer rather than a failure.** Era five changes the ground's **state** — era four's
paving failing is a stronger read than a new surface would be, because a town *"living on and
inside the previous era's stonework"* is exactly what era five is. Era six changes its **pattern
and its levels**, which is right for the era that surveys rather than builds. Era nine changes
**what the ground is laid on**, and its ground is conceptually its whole era: an era that hides
its infrastructure has a floor that is the lid over it.

**So the durable rule, for whoever authors the eleventh era: where an era has no new ground
material to reach for, change the ground's STATE, its PATTERN, or WHAT IT IS LAID ON.** All three
have been used, all three work, and all three are cheaper than inventing a material the period
did not have.

**One advance is quieter than the rest and it is 8 → 9** — both grounds hard, pale and flat, with
the change in **grain** rather than in material class. **That is a legitimate axis rather than a
weakness, and the folder has already ruled so**: era one's guide separates its hall from its
dwelling on *"one round roof against several small flat ones"* and records that **grain survives
distance better than height does**. Era nine is authored on that precedent — *era eight poured its
ground and era nine lays it*, monolith to thousands of units — and its section additionally names
the two axes where its advance is loud, because they carry more of its beat four than the paving
does: **the shoreline softens** into stepped planted terraces with reinstated shingle and reed
margins, *the first thing to shelve toward the water since the Bronze Age*, and **the planting
goes from municipal rows to lush layered drifts**. **Still the one to look at first on a dev
server**, and the only advance in the run where that is worth saying.

#### Ground versus buildings — the constraint that gets harder every era

**Every era has one body hue and the ground has to keep it legible, and under the re-dressing
ruling each era's ground carries every PREVIOUS era's buildings too.** No single guide can see
this, so it is recorded here; **each of the nine authored sections carries its own contrast note**
and this is what they add up to. *(The pass that measures the ten body hues against each other
has since run — `../2026-07-30-era-palette-ladder.md`, all 45 co-visible pairs. It moved five
values and this section is stated at them. What it does NOT measure is the ground, which is what
this section owns.)*

**Two collapse families, and they turn out to solve each other.**

- **The PALE family — eras 2, 4, 8, and 9 at a slant.** Neolithic straw-tempered brick
  `#D9C288` on worked earth, Classical limestone `#D2C8AA` on a limestone floor, Atomic concrete
  `#B9B5AC` on poured concrete. **Era eight is the sharpest instance in the run because the wall
  and the floor are one substance.** Era nine's `#96A1AB` is cool rather than pale but has the
  run's lowest-contrast body-and-accent pair.
  *(Era two joined this family on 2026-07-30 — its redesigned brick is L\* 79.1. It is the one
  pale era whose ground is deliberately DARKER than its walls, so it is the family's easiest case
  rather than another instance of the problem.)*
  *(**Era six LEFT this family and now carries a Northern Early Modern material set.** Its
  historical pale stucco was within 0.32 L\* of era four's limestone and shared its terracotta
  roof, so the two eras read alike. The live body is **warm buff lime plaster `#C7A48E` at
  L\* 69.9**, with localized hand-made brick `#B1775A`, pale dressed-stone trim `#D8CDB3`, and
  **dark slate/sheet lead roofs `#596675`**. The wall sits 20.9 L\* below the paving and 15.4
  ΔE76 from era four's body. `../2026-07-30-era-palette-ladder.md` §12 has the exhaustive
  measurement and `era-06-early-modern/style-guide.md` the ruling.)*
- **The DARK family — era 7, and era 10.** Soot-darkened brick `#8E5F4E` with a near-black cast
  iron accent `#3E4247`, on setts and clinker darkened by the same soot: **the worst single case
  in the run.** Era ten is darker still but is the one era with an **emissive** accent, so it
  solves the problem with light rather than with value.

**They rescue each other, which is the finding worth having.** Era seven's dark brick reads
superbly on era eight's pale concrete, and the pale eras — unseparable on any pale floor from
era four onward — **all read at once on era ten's dark deck.** So the accumulated city is most
legible at the two ends of the run and least legible in the middle.

**Three rules follow, and each was arrived at independently in more than one guide before being
written here:**

1. **A pale era must author its floor BELOW its wall in value.** A horizontal surface takes the
   top-face lightening in the three-tone model and will otherwise come out brighter than the thing
   standing on it. Eras four, six and eight each reached this separately.
2. **Every era carries its own dark element where the building meets the ground**, so separation
   never depends on the floor alone: era four's kerb and coping, era five's oak frame, era six's
   rusticated base, era eight's shadow gap under the floating plane, era nine's shadow gap. **Four
   consecutive eras needed one, which makes it a rule rather than four coincidences.**
3. **The later the era, the more its ground must sit MID-VALUE and LOW-CHROMA**, because it has to
   hold nine eras of accumulated body hues at once. **A ground at either extreme of value serves
   one half of the city and abandons the other.** Era one's ground could be anything; era nine's
   cannot. **Era ten is the licensed exception** — it may go to the dark extreme precisely because
   its own buildings supply their own light.

Plus, per **D-ERA28** (§5.4), **the era's ADVISOR** — the character the learner talks to is
per-era art like everything else. **There is ONE advisor, Oru, re-themed in all ten eras**: ten
dressings and ten registers, one person. The ten roles each era dresses and speaks him as are the
grain keeper (Neolithic), the weights-and-measures official (Bronze Age), the harbour architect
(Classical), the guild warden (Medieval), the architect and surveyor (Early Modern), the works
engineer (Industrial), the systems analyst (Atomic Age), the network operations lead (Information
Age) and the signal engineer (Cyberpunk). **Era one is the exception and holds no trade at all**:
it is where the learner meets him, so he is introduced as *your advisor* and nothing else — see
the 2026-08-05 ruling below for why the trade era one used to carry was retired.

### The naming rule (Gabriel, 2026-07-29)

**Every building must be immediately identifiable to the vast majority of users.** The test is
not period accuracy and not cleverness: *would a typical learner know what this building is
without being told?* Named as failing: *the drying racks, the horrea, the insula, The Goods Yard &
Cold Store, The Tithe Barn*. Named as working: *The Water Well, The Market*.

**This is the lesson-prose rule applied to the world.** `.cursor/skills/lesson-qa/SKILL.md` R23
cuts invented vocabulary because *"the learner cannot look it up, cannot meet it again, and cannot
use it to ask anyone a question"*, and R32 says a real term must be earned — **glossed or
avoided**. A building name is the strict case of R32, because **a name cannot be glossed**. There
is no sentence after it. It is one line on a buy menu or one phrase in an accessible name, heard
once, by someone who cannot ask. So for a building the two remedies collapse into one, and the
only one left is *avoid*.

Five things the pass established that are not obvious from the rule alone:

1. **Plain English is not automatically safe, and evocative English is not automatically unsafe.**
   *Drying racks* is four ordinary letters-on-the-page and still failed, because the learner
   parses the words and still does not know what the object is for. *The Night Market* and *the
   Vault* pass. The question is about the **thing the name points at**, not the etymology.
2. **A compound joining two jobs fails on structure**, whatever its words. *The Goods Yard & Cold
   Store*, *The Chapel & Well*, *The Piazza & Fountain*, *The Supermarket & Cold Chain*: pick the
   noun that carries the building and let the other half live in the art description.
3. **Civic names and subtitles are held to different bars, and the difference is mechanical.** A
   subtitle is always preceded by its stable name — a learner hears *"Dwellings,* the Stacks", so
   *Dwellings* has already told them what it is and the subtitle only owes the era. A civic
   building's name stands alone with nothing in front of it. The same word can therefore pass as a
   subtitle and fail as a civic name.
4. **A subtitle may repeat an earlier era's; a civic name may not.** The city wears one era's
   dressings at a time (`eraDressing.ts` resolves every subtitle from `CURRENT_ERA_ID`), so era
   five's Bell Tower and era six's are never both on the island. D-ERA11 freezes civic buildings
   in the era they were built, so an era-ten city holds all forty at once and those forty names
   must be unique across the whole run.

   > **REVERSED 2026-07-29 by the re-dressing ruling: point 4's first half is now FALSE and the
   > two halves have collapsed into one rule.** The premise — *the city wears one era's dressings
   > at a time* — was true only because every building re-dressed. A skill building now keeps the
   > subtitle it was built with, so **era five's Bell Tower and era six's WOULD both stand on the
   > island**, and a screen-reader user navigating by name would meet two buildings called
   > "Tower, the Bell Tower".
   >
   > **The rule is therefore: EVERY name in the game must be unique across the whole run** —
   > all 62 subtitles and all ~40 civic names, in one namespace, exactly the bar point 4 set for
   > civic names alone. **The second half of point 4 is unchanged and now simply covers more.**
   >
   > **This is the one place the ruling makes a guide's job harder rather than easier**, and it
   > is worth checking before the nine rows are wired: any two eras that reached for the same
   > obvious word for the same mass now collide, where previously they never met. **The
   > distinction in point 3 above still stands** — a subtitle is heard after its stable name, so
   > it is still held to a lower recognisability bar than a civic name. Uniqueness and
   > recognisability are different tests and only the first one changed.

5. **THE FOUR CIVIC ROLES EACH HAVE A FAMILY, AND THE BLEND'S IS THE ONE WORTH NAMING: it is
   always the small shared amenity everyone in the settlement uses.** *(Added 2026-07-29, on the
   team lead's observation while renaming era one's. It had been implicit for ten eras, which is
   exactly why it is written down: an implicit regularity cannot be reached for by whoever names
   the next one.)*

   **The lineage, read across the run:** the **Spring**, the **Village Well**, the Trading Post,
   the **Fountain**, the **Chapel** (with its well against the side wall), the Painters' School,
   the Library, the Fire Station, the **Hospital**, and the **Nightclub**. **Six of the ten are
   literally water**, which is not a coincidence: water is the amenity a settlement cannot do
   without and the one everybody walks to. **The column now opens and closes on it** — era one's
   Spring is found and era ten's Nightclub is sunk into the deck and free at the door in a city that
   meters everything else.

   > **CORRECTED by the consolidated pass, and this is the one place the lineage genuinely weakens.**
   > Era ten's blend row was **The Reservoir** when this was written, which is why the paragraph
   > could say *"six of the ten are literally water"* and *"the column now opens and closes on it"*.
   > **The lead has reinstated The Nightclub**, so the closing entry is no longer water and **five of
   > the ten are** — Spring, Village Well, Fountain, Chapel, and era nine's Hospital as the nearest
   > equivalent. **The family's real claim survives**, because it was never about water: it is *the
   > small shared amenity everyone in the settlement queues at*, and a warm room free at the door in
   > a metered city is exactly that. `era-10-cyberpunk/civic-buildings.md` states this plainly rather
   > than smoothing it, and authors a free water point at the end of the bar to carry the literal
   > thread. **The "opens and closes on water" reading is withdrawn.**

   **Why the family exists rather than being a habit.** The blend row is the only one that carries
   **both** capacities, and it is always the era's **smallest plot** — 1×1 or 1×2 in every era. A
   building that is tiny and yet worth two dials has to be something the whole settlement shares:
   a private amenity that small would serve nobody, and a large one would not be a blend. So the
   shape of the mechanic picks the institution, and the name should follow the institution.

   **How to use it.** When naming a blend building, ask *what is the small thing everyone in this
   period queued at?* — and check the answer against the two eras either side, because this family
   is where the run's collisions come from. Era six's Hospital and era nine's Clinic were the same
   institution three eras apart; era two's Village Well and a proposed era-three well would have
   been too. **The family is a source of good names and, for the same reason, of duplicates.**

   > **The corollary, since it decided one of the fifteen items:** a name that points at a
   > *different* mechanic than its row is worse than a vague one. Era one's blend was *The Storage
   > Pit* and it provides **no food at all**, so the name promised the food row's job. That is the
   > failure the naming rule's *"the thing the name points at"* test catches when it is applied to
   > the **`Provides` cell** rather than only to the words.

**Height is expressed as an ORDER, never as a world-unit target, and the reason is mechanical.**
Every construction stage is contain-fit to its plot by **one uniform scale**, so a rendered height
is `nativeHeight × FOOTPRINT_FILL × plotSpan / nativeGroundSpan` — the same model stands at two
different heights on two different plots, and the rendered width-to-height always equals the
model's own. So a number is not something the person authoring a model can aim at, and the only
lever they hold is the model's **proportion**: the more slender of two neighbours on one plot is
simply the taller one. The nine later guides therefore state a ranking — the tallest thing in the
settlement, low and long, taller than the store and shorter than the tower — which survives
re-authoring where a figure does not. **The one number that is real is the 24-unit ceiling**
(`MAX_BUILD_HEIGHT`, `GRID_CELL * 6`), because it is a cap that silently squashes anything taller,
and only era ten's mast is authored to approach it.

**A footprint change was tried and reverted on 2026-07-28, and the reasoning is worth reading before
anyone proposes another.** `NUM.5` was moved to a single tile on the theory that a smaller plot would
reinforce "slender and vertical". Per the paragraph above that is false: the Lookout renders exactly
half as tall on a single tile as on the square plot at an unchanged width-to-height, so the change
spent real height and bought no shape. It is back on the square plot, and `NODE_FOOTPRINTS`' own
header in `worldLayout.ts` now carries the measurements. `NUM.2` moving to the square plot stands,
because what a footprint genuinely controls is **how much of its plot a building fills**.

### How to write a PROHIBITION (2026-08-01)

**State a prohibition in the same words as the release it protects, in the same sentence, and
name the ROLE rather than the material.** Every guide from era two onward withholds things
because a later era announces them, so a prohibition is one half of a pair and the other half is
written in a different folder by a different hand at a different time. The two only stay the same
width if the narrower one carries its own scope where it is read.

**The failure this prevents, and it was found in eight of the nine eras that have a successor.**
A guide writes *"no structural iron"* or *"no window glass at any real size"*, the successor
releases something much narrower — iron **as structure**, glass **at the size of a composed
opening** — and the surplus in between is forbidden by the earlier guide and released by nobody.
The tell is always the same: **the ban names a MATERIAL where the release names a ROLE.** A
material is a substance and a role is a job, and no era in this run has ever actually been asked
to give up a substance.

**The worked example is era seven's cantilever sentence**, which is the one prohibition in the
folder that cannot be misread, because it restates its own scope inside itself:

> *"reinforced concrete and the cantilever, both the Atomic Age's announcement, **so nothing here
> projects without a bracket or a column under it**"*

The clause after *so* is the whole technique. It names what an artist may not **draw** rather
than what they may not **use**, it is exactly as wide as era eight's release, and an artist who
reads only that sentence and never opens era eight still draws the right building. Compare the
same guide's iron line before it was corrected on 2026-08-01 — *"iron has been held back since
era three's bronze"* — which forbade the substance, four eras wider than the rule stated one line
above it.

**The worse sub-species: the ORPHANED PROHIBITION, and it cannot be caught from inside.** A ban
can be wider than the release *and the surplus can be released by nobody at all*. Era five
forbade the large window; era six releases only the domestic rectangle in a casement; the Gothic
traceried light falls in the gap and no era in the run ever picks it up. **The only detection
mechanism this folder has is a ban colliding with a later release** — two documents contradicting
each other in a way a reader can see. A surplus nothing downstream releases collides with
nothing, so it reads as consistent forever and is invisible to any review that works by comparing
guides. **It has to be caught at the moment the prohibition is written**, which is what this rule
is for.

**So, before writing "no X" in a guide, answer three questions in the sentence itself:**

1. **Which later era releases X, and in what words?** If you cannot name the release, you are
   probably writing an orphan — stop and check what happens to the surplus.
2. **What ROLE does that release actually give X?** Ban that role, not the substance. *"No iron
   carrying the building"*, not *"no iron"*.
3. **What may an artist still draw?** Say it in the same breath. A prohibition that also states
   its remainder is one a reader cannot widen by accident.

**Correcting one in a guide that is already closed follows the folder's existing convention: add
a dated blockquote against the operative text rather than rewrite the passage.** Where the words
are quoted elsewhere, leave them standing and let the note state the role they mean — era six's
§2 does this for *"structural iron"*, because three further passages in that guide cite the
phrase. Where a clause
is not merely wide but **false**, strike the clause and record the strike in the same dated note,
so the deletion is reviewable rather than silent — era four's ground section and era five's
island section both do this for the iron tie. All three landed 2026-08-01.
