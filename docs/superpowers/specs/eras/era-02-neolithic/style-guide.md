## 1. The era in one line, and its signature move

**One line:** a settlement that has started to **work the land** — plots, furrows,
boundaries, penned animals and stored grain — built in moulded brick and thatch.

**The signature move — the WORKED LAND.** Era one lived *on* the ground; this era does
something *to* it. Turf is stripped, stones are picked out and heaped at a plot's edge,
soil is broken and raked into furrows, seed goes in, a boundary is put round it, animals
are shut inside a fence, and the crop comes off the field and into a pit, a basket and a
store. **Every one of those is a change to the ground itself**, which is why the era's
signature is delivered in the island section rather than in the architecture — see **The
island (D-ERA26)** below, which is this guide's most load-bearing section and is written to
the same depth as §2 and §3.

**Why it suits this era specifically, and why nothing else does.** The Neolithic Revolution
**is** agriculture. It is also, uniquely in this run, an era whose whole identity is legible
without any architectural literacy at all: a field beside unbroken ground is a thing anybody
recognises, where *round poles becoming square corners* is a distinction only an architect
draws. And it is **already authored everywhere except in what the player sees** — the era's
resource is **grain** (`civic-buildings.md` §1), its buildings are a Granary, a Seed House,
a Bread Oven and a Sheep Pen, its advisor is a grain keeper, and its lesson framings run
on the cleared field and the seed held back to sow. The farming was in the content and was
not the headline. It is the headline now.

**The building language, and it is unchanged: the moulded unit.** The sun-dried brick is the
first repeated, identical component anybody on this island has made. Era one shaped every
piece to the place it went: a post to its hole, a stone to its gap, a hide to its frame. Here
a mould is filled a hundred times and the hundred results are interchangeable, so **ornament
stops being the notch and becomes the course.** The era's decorative language is the way the
unit is laid — regular horizontal banding, a projecting course under an eave, a herringbone
panel in a gable, a row of headers set to throw a shadow line. All of it is structural, which
is what keeps it legible at zoom-out and what keeps it inside D-ERA8: these people pattern
everything they build this way, and the pattern says nothing about the lesson indoors.

**And the brick follows from the fields rather than standing beside them**, which is the
right way round and is the one thing the re-scheme genuinely rewrites. Repetition is what
agriculture actually feels like — the same furrow, the same seed, the same measure of grain,
a hundred times over — so **the mould is the field's habit carried indoors.** A settlement
that has learned to do one thing a hundred times identically in a field is the settlement
that will do it in a wall, and era one's silhouettes all differ slightly from one another
precisely because nobody there had yet had to. *(This paragraph argued the same causation in
the opposite direction until 2026-07-30, when the brick was the headline and agriculture was
its justification.)*

**The tally descendant: impressed clay tokens and a tablet.** Era one predicts the motif's
own chain — notches on a post become a chalked slate becomes a printed ledger becomes a
display panel — and this is the second link in it. The notch is now pressed into soft clay
instead of cut into wood: a flat palm-sized tablet with rows of impressed marks, and
beside it a bowl of small clay tokens, one to a thing counted. The tally post itself does
not vanish from the island. It stands weathered and unused **beside the landmark**, which is
the cheapest way there is to show that counting has moved off the woodwork and into the
hand. *(Re-pointed 2026-07-30: this read "near the bake house", which was the landmark's
withdrawn form. **The prop survives the re-author unchanged** and §5's `town-hall` entry
still carries it.)*

## 3. Structural language

**Buildings stand up by being walls**, and every other change in the era follows from that
one. Era one propped and leaned, with the thing carrying the load on the outside where it
could be seen. Here a wall carries its own roof, the frame moves inside, and the surface
becomes the building.

- **Load-bearing mass.** Brick walls, thick at the base, with era one's batter much
  reduced — present enough to keep the family resemblance, gone as a necessity, because a
  moulded unit stacks plumb.
- **The course line, everywhere.** Horizontal, unbroken, and the era's strongest read at
  distance. Where a building wants emphasis it gets more course lines, not more shapes.
- **Plans that close.** Rectangles with square corners and true circular drums, rather
  than era one's approximations of both. A room is now a room with a door rather than a
  shelter with a way in.
- **Openings small and few.** A timber lintel over a narrow door, one high opening where
  light or air is wanted. **Nothing spans a wide gap**, because there is no arch until the
  Classical era and no timber long enough to fake one.
- **Roofs by plan.** Thatch ridges over the rectangles, low domed caps of clay over reed
  on the drums, and flat mud roofs on close-set beams over the dwelling cells — the first
  usable roof surface in the run.

  > **EXTENDED 2026-07-30, and the extension is the point rather than a loosening.** The flat mud
  > roof reached only the dwelling cells; the two buildings re-authored on 2026-07-30 both take one,
  > for opposite reasons. **`EXP.1`'s is hidden** behind a brick parapet, so the roof is not part of
  > the read and the block stays a prism. **The landmark's IS the read** — one unbroken plane with a
  > single square smoke opening, and the first roof in the run used as a *civic* floor rather than a
  > domestic one. **The three uses stay distinguishable**, which is the property this bullet owes: a
  > terrace you live on, a parapet you cannot see over, and a floor the settlement stands on.

- **The floor lifts clear of the ground.** The raised store on stone stumps is the era's
  structural invention and the genuinely clever thing in it: a building deliberately not
  touching the soil, because what is inside has to stay dry. *(Narrowed 2026-07-30: this used to be
  true of two buildings and is now true of **one**. `EXP.1` was withdrawn as a raised store — see
  §5 — so **the civic Granary is the era's only lifted building**, which makes the lift its identity
  rather than a shared move. The invention stands; it is now made once.)*
- **Ground worked and swept, not paved.** Packed earth, beaten hardest where it is used
  hardest. **This is the ground the buildings stand ON, and it is deliberately the pale
  half of the island** — the broken and sown ground is in the margin around them. The
  island section below owns that split and the reason for it.

**Heights are stated as an order, and this guide deliberately gives no world-unit targets.**
A rendered height is not something the person authoring a model can aim at: every stage is
contain-fit to its plot by **one uniform scale**, so the same model stands at two different
heights on two different plots, and the only thing an author actually controls is the
model's own proportion. What an era owes is therefore the **ranking**, which survives
re-authoring where a number does not.

**This era's order, tallest first:** the granary tower, standing clear of everything rather
than merely beating it; then the granary rotunda, the tallest of the roofed buildings; then
**the landmark, which is wide rather than tall and whose only vertical is its screen wall**;
then the dwelling cells and the firing yard, close together and low; **then `EXP.1`'s block,
lower than it used to be**; and **both yard-mass buildings lowest** — the chain's threshing
floor and RAD.2's sheep pen — reading as ground with things standing on it.

> **RE-ORDERED 2026-07-30, and the movement is downwards in both cases.** The order read *"then
> the bake house, which is wide rather than tall; then the raised grain store; then the dwelling
> cells and the firing yard"*. **Both of those buildings were withdrawn by Evan** (§5): the
> landmark is now a sunken hearth room whose walls stand only shoulder-high, so its height is the
> screen wall rather than a canopy on posts; and `EXP.1` is now a block standing on the ground,
> where **all of the old store's height was in its stumps**, so it drops past the dwelling cells
> and the firing yard to sit just above the yards. **The bottom of the range still does not
> move**, and the top is untouched. **The era as a whole is a step taller than era
one** and still leaves most of the headroom under the 24-unit ceiling unused — only era
ten's mast is authored to approach that. **The bottom of the range does not move at all**,
because the bottom is the yard and the yard never rises in any of the ten eras. *(Re-authoring
RAD.2 from a threshing circle to a sheep pen on 2026-07-29 did not lift it: a pen is a boundary
rather than a building, and nothing in it stands higher than the hurdling.)*

## 4. Ornament and visible technology

**Ornament level: low, and it is the course.** Everything decorative is a decision about
how to lay the unit — a projecting band under the eaves, a line of headers turned to catch
a shadow, a herringbone panel filling a gable, a moulded clay finial where a ridge ends, a
daubed panel left lighter than the brick around it. No applied pattern that is not also a
way of building, no carving, no painted field.

**Visible technology, which is where the era earns its name:** the mould and the drying
floor, the kiln, the quern, the storage pit with its woven lid, the ladder, the penning
hurdle, grain in the ear and grain in the basket. **Fire is enclosed here for the first
time** — an oven and a kiln, with mouths and flues, where era one had an open ring of
stones — and that enclosure is the era's clearest technological step, because a mouth and a
flue can be seen from outside the building.

**Nothing that implies metal.** No blade, no fitting, no pin, no sheet, no bright edge
anywhere. Joints are still lashing and pegs, cutting is still stone. The Bronze Age has to
be able to arrive, and it can only arrive if this era is scrupulous.

**The wheel, precisely.** Era one reserves the wheel for this era along with cultivated
grain, and this era should take only half of it: **rotation, not transport.** The potter's

bookkeeping one: nothing era two draws puts the climb back.)*

**RAD.4 is named *the Stone Tower*, and the name must not be read as a description of the
fabric.** The lead locked it on 2026-07-29 and the subtitle row carries the ruling in full. **The
building is a mud-brick drum with stone only at its footing, and it is deliberately not tall.**
The name is about the **stone footing the drum stands on and the massiveness of the drum
itself** — a mass you fill rather than a frame you climb — and nothing in it licenses a stone
shaft: §2's rule that undressed stone stays at footing level and is *never coursed above the
ground in this era* is unchanged and still binds this building. Model the squat mud-brick drum
described above; **do not model stone courses above the footing and do not make it tall.**

**Chain flag — the tower chain breaks here, and the break should be recorded rather than
smoothed over.** Era one's through-line is *an open frame you climb*, and a granary tower
is a solid drum. The frame is gone, the climb has moved inside, and the read era one
designed for — empty air above a small base — is contradicted outright. It matters more
than a local mismatch would, because the tower is the strongest chain in the run: it opens
as an open frame in era one and closes as an open frame in era ten, by way of an
eighth-era lattice pylon that deliberately opens it up again. **RESOLVED 2026-07-29 as proposed:
keep the ladder and the head platform expressed on the outside of the drum**, so the climb
stays visible from every azimuth and the silhouette still reads as something you go up
rather than something you fill. **What is lost is seeing *through* it**, and that loss is what
makes this a break rather than a re-dressing; the closure block at the end of this guide states
it in full. From here it becomes a gate tower with an
external timber walkway, then a harbour tower with an open colonnaded top stage, then a
belfry, a campanile, an iron tank stage, a lattice pylon, and a mast.

**Landmark (`town-hall`) — the sunken hearth room.** *Through-line: a sheltered communal
fire.* The wide plot. **A communal hearth room dug a step into the earth, walled in
straw-tempered brick, and roofed flat on close-set beams so that the roof is where the
settlement stands.** The walls are the retaining walls of that room and rise only
shoulder-high above the outside ground; the roof is one unbroken plane of packed mud carrying
**a single square opening over the fire**, kerbed in brick, which is where the smoke leaves
and where the fire is seen from above. **A broad brick stair descends into the room on one
long side**, wide enough for several people abreast — the width *is* the authority. **One
broad brick screen wall stands above the roof line** behind the opening, carrying an unbroken
projecting course and one herringbone daub panel, with the smoke rising in front of it: the
biggest decorated wall in the era, on the era's biggest plot. Below, a long fire bed of fired
clay set into the floor, brick benches down both long walls, plaster worn dark with smoke.
**Wide rather than tall — the authority is plan area and the screen, never height**, and the
building sits above the store and below the rotunda.
**The fire has come indoors**, which is this era's clearest reading of the chain: era one's
ring of standing stones was open to the sky over its hearth, and era two has put walls all
round that fire and a roof over the whole of it, leaving one square hole. It becomes
a walled furnace court in the Bronze Age, a colonnaded public hearth in the Classical era,
then a guildhall with a roof louvre, a council hall of clustered chimneystacks, an engine
house carrying the island's tallest chimney, a heating plant showing only its plume, a
data hall whose fire is invisible, and a lit plant stack exhaling steam through gratings
that people still gather at. **The fire never goes out; it only becomes harder to see.**

**This is the first landmark in the era built of what the era is made of**, and that is the
reason it was re-authored. The withdrawn design carried a fired-clay dome, a thatch canopy,
timber posts, an earth platform and a stone edge — **no mud brick anywhere**, which is why
`output/imagegen/era-02-neolithic-building-briefs/README.md` had to state *"the course does not
apply here, since there is no brick wall."* It applies now: twelve units of coursed brick on
each long face, plus the screen.

## The island (D-ERA26) — and this is where era two's identity lives

**This section carries the era's signature move (§1), so it is authored to the depth §2 and §3
are authored to rather than as scene-setting.** The era the ground stops being walked on and
starts being **worked**: broken, sown, bounded, fenced and harvested. Still the same
soft-edged island, and the water's edge is deliberately almost untouched — **era two spends its
whole change budget inland**, which is what leaves the Bronze Age its quay.

**Against era one, as era one authors itself, and era one is not re-specified here.** Its §6b
reads *"Packed bare earth and dry tussock grass, worn through to soil along the routes people
walk. **Nothing planted, nothing in rows, nothing fenced** — cultivation is era two's premise,
not era one's"*, with vegetation *"sparse and windblown … nothing ornamental and nothing in
flower"* and a read of *"a shoreline camp that stayed long enough to become a settlement, with
the land not yet caught up."* **Era one's ground is one state; era two's is four at once.**
That is the whole of the transition and everything below is its detail.

### Ground — the worked plot, and it is four surfaces rather than one

**The island reads in two families for the first time: ground somebody has broken, and ground
nobody has touched.** The untouched half is era one's exactly and is not re-authored — pale dry
tussock over packed earth, bare rock still showing on the high ground.

**The worked half is divided into PLOTS, and the plot is the era's unit of ground** the way the
brick is its unit of wall. A plot is small — the area one household can break by hand in a
season — irregular in outline, and its shape is argued from the slope and from where the stones
ran out rather than from a survey. **They are not the same size and no two are the same shape.**
A dozen or so is the right number; enough to read as a farmed settlement, few enough that each
is legibly somebody's.

**A settlement's fields are never all in one state, and authoring all four at once is what makes
the island read as farmed rather than as a green patch.** This is the single most useful thing
in this section for whoever models it:

| State | What the surface is | Tone |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Freshly broken** | Soil turned and raked into **furrows**, clods still visible, no growth | The darkest ground on the island, and damp-looking |
| **Standing crop** | Cereal to knee or waist height, dense, hiding the soil entirely | The palest and the only tall ground surface |
| **Stubble** | Cut stalks a hand high in rows, husk drifted between them, soil showing through | Mid, and the only ground that reads **striped** |
| **Fallow** | Broken once and left; weeds coming back through it unevenly | Between worked and wild, and the state that makes the other three legible |

**Furrows, precisely, because this is where an anachronism gets in.** They are **hoed and dug,
never ploughed** — see the absent-list check below — so they are **short, shallow, crooked and
interrupted**, they stop where a stone was and resume past it, and they follow the plot's
outline rather than running its full length. At the fixed ~49° camera a furrow is a
fine-pitched corrugation catching one tone step, and the direction changes plot to plot: **no
two adjacent plots are furrowed the same way**, which is what stops a dozen fields reading as
one striped carpet.

**One patch of ground is worked and NOT sown, and it is the era's threshing floor.** §7 rules
that threshing lives as props and worked ground rather than as a building, so the island owes
it a place: **a patch beaten bare, swept hard and pale, with the sledge parked on it and husk
drifted off its downwind edge.** It sits between the plots and the settlement, because it is
where the crop stops being a field and starts being a store.

### Field boundaries — low, made of what came out of the ground, and never built

**Everything that divides this island came out of the plot it divides**, and that is the rule
rather than a description. Three kinds and no more:

- **Stone clearance heaps lengthening into rough walls.** These are the primary boundary and
  they are **heaps that have grown along**, not walls that were built: no courses, no faces, no
  through-stones, no gateposts. Knee-high, wider at the base than a built wall would be, taller
  where the picking was worse, and **petering out entirely where a plot's stones ran out** —
  which is the detail that says *made of what the field gave up* louder than any amount of
  texture.
- **Brushwood and stake lines**, where there was no stone: cut scrub jammed between driven
  stakes, leaning, gappy, and obviously renewed in patches.
- **A scratched ditch** where a plot meets a path or a wet corner needs draining. **Unlined,
  a scratch rather than a cut**, with the spoil thrown up on one side as a low bank.

**Gateways are gaps, not openings.** A hurdle panel dragged across, a stone left out of the
heap, a trodden gap. Nothing is hung on anything but the sheep pen's own gate of lashed poles.

**Nothing encloses the whole island.** The boundaries are around plots, not around the
settlement — there is no wall, no palisade, no defensive line, and nothing that reads as a
perimeter. This era has decided where things grow; it has not yet decided who is outside.

### Vegetation — two kinds, and their separation IS the era

**Wild:** scrub, coarse grass, the same wind-shaped trees at the margins, reeds in the
shallows — era one's planting, unchanged and now doing a new job, because it is the control
against which the sown ground reads.

**Sown:** cereal, in the lumpy hand-sown patches above. **Tall, pale and gone to seed head at
the top**, standing well clear of everything around it — this is the only vegetation on the
island with a flat top, and that flatness at zoom-out is what reads as *crop* before any
detail resolves.

**No orchards, no hedgerows, no rows of trees, no planted tree of any kind.** Every tree on
this island is where a seed landed. Deliberate tree planting is era three's — its island puts
*"the first **planted** trees — set where somebody wanted shade, not where seed happened to
land"* — and era two taking it would spend the Bronze Age's move an era early.

### Shoreline — deliberately almost unchanged, and that is a decision

Still **no built edge**, because the built edge is the Bronze Age's single most important move
and era two must not soften it. What era two adds is the first **intentional** touch and
nothing more: gravel raked flatter where boats come in, and a line of larger stones **set** at
the top of the beach to stop the bank being cut back. **Set, not dressed and not coursed** —
recognisably somebody's work, recognisably not masonry. The shelving gravel and the loose
boulder clusters survive from era one untouched.

### Where boats land

The gravel slipway is now worn broad and has a **timber skid track** laid down it — split logs,
half-sunk, to drag hulls over. Two or three hide boats above the waterline, one upturned for
repair. **Still no jetty.**

### Water

Pale and shallow at the edge as before, and now **worked for the first time**: **fish traps in
the shallows**, woven withy baskets and a low stake line running out from the beach, visible
under the surface. The water itself is unchanged in colour and depth — era two farms the water
the same way it farms the land, by putting something in it, not by altering it.

> **⚑ THIS PARAGRAPH ASKS FOR TWO THINGS THAT CANNOT BOTH HOLD, and the traps as built depart
> from it. Ruled by Evan, 2026-07-31; recorded here rather than resolved in the prose.**
>

> and depth"*. **In the renderer those are contradictory.** `apps/web/src/city/three/StarterIsland.tsx`

### Signs of habitation at the margins

Grain drying on racks. A saddle quern and a rotary quern with husk scattered round them.
**Storage pits with woven lids, at two diameters**, and the spoil from digging them still
heaped beside the newest. Sheaves stood in a stook. A raised timber platform with sacks on it.
The midden is bigger than era one's and now has **potsherds** in it, which is the cheapest way
to date the settlement from its rubbish. A penned corner of scrub with a brushwood fence,
holding a few animals — the island's echo of `RAD.2`, and it should read as the same technology
at a smaller size.

### Against the roads (D-ERA30) — and this is one decision made in two places

**The hollow way is the ROAD, and it is already authored as data**, not prose: `ERA_TWO_HOLLOW`
and `ERA_TWO_VERGE` in `apps/web/src/city/roadSurfacing.ts` give era two a scuffed verge at
`#BE9464` over a sunken tread at `#7C5C37`. **This section used to open by authoring the same
hollow way in prose** — *"the worn paths of era one are now trodden into shallow hollow ways"* —
which is the ground and the roads deciding the same surface twice in two documents. **It does
not any more, and the folder-wide rule is at `../README.md`:** the island owns the ground
**between** the roads and states its **relation** to them; D-ERA30 owns the road tile itself.

**Era two's relation, stated once.** The road tread is darker than every worked surface on the
island including freshly broken soil, and the verge is lighter than the tussock — so a route
reads **sunken** and a field reads **flat**, which is the difference between ground people walk
on and ground people work. A furrowed plot and a hollow way are both linear and dark, and that
two-sided value relation is the only thing keeping them apart at zoom-out. **Do not let a
furrow get as dark as a tread.**

### What the era's own absent list rules out of the ground

**§2's "explicitly absent" paragraph binds the island exactly as it binds a wall**, and §4's
*"nothing that implies metal"* and *"rotation, not transport"* bind it too. Checked item by
item, here is what it costs the fields — each of these is something a Neolithic farming scene
would otherwise reach for:

- **No plough, and this is the big one.** Metal is absent outright, so no share and no coulter
  — but the deeper constraint is that **this era authors no draught animal**. Its only livestock
  is `RAD.2`'s flock and the island's *penned corner … holding a few animals*, and the ox that
  once appeared here left with the threshing circle (§5). **So the ground is broken by hand**:
  the digging stick era one already has, and a hoe of stone, antler or wood. That is why the
  furrows above are short, shallow and crooked, and it is the reason era three's plots can be
  *"larger and more regular"* — the regularity arrives with the traction, not with the taste.
- **No metal sickle.** The harvest is cut with a **flint-bladed sickle** — a curved wood or
  antler haft with flint blades set in resin — which is consistent with §4's *cutting is still
  stone* and with era one's flint chips on the same ground.
- **No cart anywhere near a field.** §4 gives this era **rotation and not transport**; the
  wheeled cart is era three's authored first. Sheaves are carried on a back, dragged on a
  hurdle, or moved in baskets.
- **No built field wall.** §2 confines undressed stone to **footing level** and says it is
  *never coursed above the ground in this era*, and lime mortar and dressed ashlar are
  Classical. **A boundary here is a heap, never masonry** — era three is where they are
  *"rebuilt as proper walls … coursed, straight-ish, and waist-high"*, and era two pre-empting
  that would flatten the very next transition.
- **No managed water.** No leat, no lined channel, no sluice and no gate. A ditch is a scratch
  that drains a wet corner. Irrigation is engineering and this era has none.
- **No fired surface on the ground.** Fired roof tile is Classical, and this era's fired clay
  is a **component** material at object scale — a trough, a jar, a sherd. Nothing fired is ever
  laid as a surface.

### Contrast against the buildings, and it gets harder every era

**The risk is real and specific here, and it is worth stating because era two is where the
cumulative problem starts.** Era two's body hue is **straw-tempered sun-dried brick
`#D9C288`** and era one's buildings — which stand on this ground permanently under the
re-dressing ruling — are **terracotta `#C98A5E`**. The two now separate (ΔE76 25.24, and
18.28 in situ), but the ground question is unchanged and is the reason this paragraph
survives the palette move: **freshly broken soil authored as a rich dark brown would put a
generation of brown buildings and a generation of pale ones on brown ground, and kill era
one's silhouette outright.**

**Two things resolve it, and the second is mechanical rather than a matter of care.**

1. **The worked ground earns its read from VALUE CONTRAST against the pale tussock, not from
   absolute darkness.** It is *darker than the tussock beside it*, which is all it has to be.
   A plot is legible because of what it is next to.
2. **The plots live in the margin, and the buildings do not stand on them.** Per D-ERA26's own
   measurement of the shipped island, decoration sits **outside `BUILDABLE_RADIUS + 3`**, so the
   fields occupy the ring that already holds era one's scatter while the buildable disc keeps
   the pale worked-and-swept ground §3 specifies. **The buildings therefore stand on the pale
   ground and the fields surround them**, which is both what a real settlement looks like and
   what keeps ten eras of accumulating brown buildings legible.

**That pass has since run, and its numbers are in
`../../2026-07-30-era-palette-ladder.md`.** All ten body hues are measured there against
each other over all 45 co-visible pairs; what this section owns is the constraint the
*ground* places on them, which the ladder does not measure. Against §1.1's parchment
`#ECE4D3` the new body sits at ΔE76 **25.72** and era one's terracotta at 41.52, so neither
generation dissolves into the pale disc they stand on.

> **⚑ THIS SECTION NAMES A BODY HUE THE REST OF THIS GUIDE NO LONGER USES — flagged on the
> 2026-07-30 forward-merge, and deliberately not resolved here.** The paragraph above states era
> two's body as **`#B4714F`**, which was true when it was written and is the value still shipped in
> code. **§2 of this same guide now proposes `#D9C288`**, with the ΔE working in the palette
> section. The two statements text-merged without a git conflict because they live in different
> sections, so nothing announced the disagreement.
>
> **Left standing rather than rewritten**, because the palette proposal is one of the open
> decisions awaiting the team lead and a merge is not the place to ratify it. The argument this
> paragraph makes — that the worked ground earns its read from *value contrast* against the pale
> tussock, and that the buildings stand on the pale disc rather than on the plots — **survives
> either body value**, and survives `#D9C288` more comfortably than `#B4714F`, since a paler,
> yellower body moves further from era one's terracotta rather than nearer. **What does not
> survive is the hex in the sentence**, and it should be corrected to whichever value is ratified.

**The read:** a settlement that has started to farm and has not yet started to build. The
island is being *used* deliberately for the first time — but everything on it could still be
taken apart by hand in a day.

## The era's palette (PROPOSED — D-ERA27)

**Status: PROPOSED for Evan, not decided.** Per **D-ERA27** hue is an **era** property rather than a
domain one, so this era owns a palette. Derived from the material language §2 now carries, and
sitting on §1.1's calm parchment base — `#ECE4D3` ground, `#D9CCB2` deep ground, `#8FA9B8`
slate-mist water — rather than competing with it. **Evan asked for something to work from, not a
ruling**, so this section gives him a measured proposal, a measured alternative, and the arithmetic
for both.

| Role | Proposal | Hex |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Body hue** | Sun-dried brick, straw-tempered | `#D9C288` |
| Accent | Fired clay | `#A2503A` |

**The intent, in one line:** *era one builds with the ground it walks on, and era two digs below it,
tempers what it finds with the crop, and moulds it into a unit — so the walls stop being the colour
of the earth and become the colour of the harvest.* The subject has not moved and the settlement has
not moved; **the substance of the wall has**, which is what §2 now authors — and which the rationale
this section carried until 2026-07-30 explicitly refused to let the palette show.

> **⚑ TWO ARGUMENTS ABOUT THIS PALETTE ARE ON THE RECORD, AND NEITHER SITS BESIDE THE LIVE HUE ANY
> LONGER.** Both are moved rather than cut, and both are quoted **verbatim** in the withdrawn-text
> appendix at the end of this file, as **A2** and **A3**.
>
> - **A2, the rationale withdrawn 2026-07-30.** It argued FOR the resemblance Evan is objecting to,
>   and it got what it asked for: measured in CIELAB, era one → era two was **the smallest adjacent
>   body-hue step in the entire ten-era run at ΔE 10.1**. It is load-bearing and cannot be deleted —
>   **`D-ERA36`'s thresholds are argued against it.**
> - **A3, the team lead's argument that hue was the wrong axis**, overruled by Evan on the lead's own
>   stated condition once he had seen the delivered art. Measurement partly vindicated it: **§5a
>   finds only two of the five permanent skill buildings read as square-cornered prisms**, so the
>   axis the lead was defending is the one the art under-delivers. **Round becoming square remains
>   the primary axis and §5a treats it as such; hue is added as a second axis, never substituted for
>   the first** — the brief README's previous-era column gives the reason to keep it second, that a
>   colour is the first thing an art pass can undo.

**Both values are Evan's, authored 2026-07-30, and this section holds them fixed.** They
arrived from a separate lane at `a14955c` with their own CIE76 derivation, and four figures
recorded there depend on hues the ladder pass below moves. The ordering ruling is that **the
ladder re-derives against these two, not the other way round** — the reasoning, and the
values the ladder pass had proposed instead, are in
`../../2026-07-30-era-palette-ladder.md` §5a.

**Why this hue, and why it is no longer `#B4714F`.** §2's Materials table gives the body to
the **sun-dried** brick and confines fired clay to components — *"never a whole wall."* The
retired value `#B4714F` was picked for **fired** brick, and the 2026-07-29 ruling below
renamed the label while deliberately leaving the hex alone, so the era's body hue went on
describing a material the era does not build walls out of. **Terracotta red is what clay
becomes in a fire.** A brick of dug clay heavily tempered with chopped straw and dried in
the sun is a pale straw-buff, dusty and desaturated, and it is paler than the ground it came
out of because the temper is pale and the water has left it. `#D9C288` is not a colour
chosen to be different from era one; it is **the colour era two's authored body material
actually has.**

**Why it is era two's and cannot be era one's.** Era one's rammed earth is the ground as
found — one substance, one colour, whatever the topsoil is. Era two's brick is **two
substances mixed on purpose**, clay dug from below the worked soil and straw off the
harvest, which makes it the first building material in the run that is **part crop**. So the
walls stop being the colour of the earth and become the colour of the harvest. That is
unavailable to era one at any price, because era one has no harvest. That is also the era's
own premise (§1: *"repetition is what agriculture actually feels like"*) arriving in the
fabric rather than only in the props.

**Why fired clay takes the accent.** §2 authors fired clay as **"the era's signature"** and
the palette had it nowhere: the accent was reed thatch, which §2 gives as era one's material
*"exactly as in era one."* So the era's signature material was invisible in its palette and
its predecessor's was the accent. An accent is component-scale by definition, so promoting
fired clay to it costs §2's *"never a whole wall"* nothing — it puts the note on the oven
mouth, the kiln lining, the trough, the ridge finial and the door plaque, every place §2
already puts fired clay, and makes the era's one genuinely new-to-the-run material its most
saturated note.

**The accent moved for a measured reason and not only a thematic one.** Against the new
body, the retired reed thatch `#C2AE83` is ΔE76 **10.44** as a swatch and **7.69** in situ

## 6. The three construction stages

Each of the eleven buildings gets three models. The stage names are fixed in code
(`buildingAssets.ts:41`), and the era's job is to make the sequence read as *this era's*
way of building.

| Stage | Engine states | Neolithic read |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **`site`** | `not_started`, `preview` | Turf stripped to bare soil in the footprint's shape and a shallow trench dug for the first course — and beside it **brick moulds and a drying floor of green bricks laid out in rows**, turned to take the sun. Flat: ankle-height at most, nothing that reads as a building yet |
| **`construction`** | `teaching`, `practice` | Walls rising in courses off **an earth ramp, with no scaffold at all**, because the ramp is how a wall this thick gets built. The timber roof plate stacked on the ground where it will be lifted from, thatch bundles cut but not yet laid |
| **`built`** | `unfinished`, `complete`, full mastery | The finished building as described in §5 and in the roster. `advanced` (mastery ≥ 2) and full mastery both reuse this model — there is no separate gilded version, and mastery rides above it as stars (`buildingAssets.ts:28-31`) |

**The site stage is this era's unmistakable image, and it deserves more effort than the
other two.** Nobody in era one had a mould, so a rectangle of green bricks drying in rows
beside a trench dates the settlement on its own, with no building above ground yet to help
it. It is also the stage seen before anything has been earned, which makes it the era's
first impression as often as the finished architecture is.

**The absence of scaffold is a positive choice rather than a saving.** Era three brings
shear-legs and a rope, era four brings a treadwheel crane standing on the wall head.
Building off a heaped earth ramp is the honest Neolithic answer, and it should look like
effort: the ramp is bigger than the wall it serves.

> ## ⚑ WHERE THE THRESHING WENT, and why it is a prop rather than a building
>
> **The question, asked because it is the one the re-author could have got badly wrong.** Era
> two's resource is **grain** (`civic-buildings.md` §1) and threshing is how grain is actually
> got off the stalk. RAD.2 was the only building in the era that did it, so re-authoring it as a
> sheep pen takes a step out of the middle of the era's own material chain: **harvest → thresh →
> winnow → store → grind → bake**. Everything either side of the missing step is authored and
> visible — sheaves stood in a stook and grain drying on racks before it; the advisor's **winnowing
> basket** immediately after it; the raised store, the grain drum and the civic Granary holding
> it; the querns and the oven at the end. Without threshing the era shows a settlement whose
> grain arrives already loose in the basket, which is the kind of gap that makes a resource feel
> asserted rather than produced.
>
> **What was checked before concluding it is a hole.** §5's yard-mass **chain instance is still
> a threshing floor** and keeps the word, but it is REFERENCE ONLY under the 2026-07-29
> re-dressing ruling and **will never be built**, so it cannot be the era's answer. §3's height
> ranking names it, and so do eras eight, nine and ten in the yard-never-rises list — all of
> those are the chain instance too, and all stay correct. Nothing else in the era threshes: the
> civic **Granary** stores, the **Bread Oven** bakes, the querns grind, and neither the hub nor
> the roster's other four touch the stalk. So the hole is real and it is one step wide.
>
> **The recommendation, and no building is proposed.** The roster is one building per node and
> fixed (D-ERA7), so threshing should live as **props and worked ground on buildings that
> already stand**, in three places the era has already authored:
>
> 1. **The props kit, above** — a threshing sledge and a patch of ground beaten bare and swept
>    for it. Threshing is done on ground rather than in a structure, so a prop is not a
>    consolation prize here; it is what the activity actually is.
> 2. **The ground around the hub**, which is the natural host and needs nothing invented. §5
>    already gives that plot communal querns, sheaves stood in a stook and flour worked pale into
>    the surface people stand on — the whole of the era's communal food work bar this one step. A
>    beaten patch with the sledge parked on it completes the sequence on the one plot that already
>    reads as where the settlement's grain is dealt with. *(Re-pointed 2026-07-30: this read "the
>    hub's apron … querns set round the platform", against the withdrawn domed-oven form. The
>    re-authored hub has no apron and no platform; it has a roof the settlement stands on, with the

> 3. **The island's margins**, §"Signs of habitation", which already carry grain drying on racks
>    and a quern with husk scattered round it. Husk drifting off a beaten patch belongs in the
>    same list.
>
> **Owner: whoever draws the hub and the island.** Recorded here rather than actioned into
> either, because the hub's form was not that pass's to move. *(The hub's name was also unresolved
> when this was written. It is settled — **the Elders' Hearth**, authored in §5. The form HAS since

## Lesson framings

**What this section is.** The era folder carries lesson framing (team lead, 2026-07-29) — how a
lesson sets its mathematics inside the era's world. It replaces the retired island themes spec.

**This era's lessons are ALREADY WRITTEN**, so this section **records** rather than invents.
`content/lessons/d2/` holds all five packs (Gabe, PRs #53 and #57, quality-remediated through
#65, #92 and eight further rounds). Where a row below and a shipped pack disagree, **read the
pack**.

**The single most useful fact about those packs, and the reason O-11 costs no content rework:**
they are deliberately **light on setting**. The plain-language pass exists because forced boomtown
theming made D2 unreadable, and the packs settled on everyday nouns that lead with the
mathematics — *a square patch of ground, a wooden beam, a rope, a shop's takings week on week*.
Three of those four are as Neolithic as they are frontier. **The re-theme is a handful of nouns,
not a rewrite**, and the direction is toward the era's own material rather than toward more
flavour.

> **The handful is countable, so here it is, measured rather than estimated (2026-07-29).** The
> only thing in those packs that names a world that no longer exists is the place name itself:
> **"Boomtown Square" appears in four of the five packs**, once each, in the **opening paragraph**
> — `EXP.1.json` (the market's crowd doubling), `RAD.1.json` (a builder fencing a square garden),
> `RAD.2.json` (the timber yard stacking boards) and `RAD.3.json` (the cube-shaped water tank).
> `RAD.4.json` names no place at all. Four surrounding nouns want the same pass: the water tank,
> the timber yard, the clerk's stock book, and fence ordered by the metre.
>
> **Owed by Gabe**, since D2 is his: one noun per opener, no maths, no answer key and no item
> touched. **Not done in the pass that measured it**, deliberately — lesson JSON is not an inert
> file, so it pulls the full test suite, and a content re-skin deserves its own validator run
> rather than riding along with a documentation diff.

**Three rules the era imposes.**

1. **The framing comes from the era's WORLD, not the node's BUILDING** (D-ERA8).
2. **§2's "Explicitly absent" list is a hard boundary.** **No metal of any kind** — that is the
   Bronze Age's whole identity and its first appearance has to be legible — and no lime mortar, no
   true arch, no dressed ashlar, no fired roof tile, no glass. A word problem may not contain a
   coin, a blade, a nail or a scale with a metal beam.
3. **Theming follows the ERA, not the domain.** This era is one whole domain, so nothing splits.

**The era's context palette — six reusable settings, from this guide's own props:** the **cleared
field** and what it yields; the **seed grain** held back to sow; the **granary** and what it
holds; the **mud brick** as a unit that tiles a wall; the **firing pit** and its batches; and the
**cord and stake** used to set out a square on the ground.

**The brick and the square patch of ground are this era's two best objects and they do most of the
work.** A brick is a unit square you can count along a wall in two directions, which is what makes
area a product; a cord pegged to a stake sweeps a square you can pace out. Both are in the era's
own material language, both are already close to what the shipped packs say, and between them they
cover four of the five nodes without reaching for anything the era does not have.

| Node | The mathematics | Neolithic framings (2 to 3 per node) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **EXP.1** — exponent laws: product, power, zero and negative exponents | growth by repeated multiplication | (1) **Seed that multiplies season on season** — the era design's own phrase, and the cleanest exponential in the game: each planting returns some multiple of what was sown. (2) **A store that doubles**, and what "doubling stops" and "doubling reverses" look like as a zero and a negative exponent. | **RAD.2** — operations with radicals | combining sides and areas | (1) **Two square plots side by side**, and what can and cannot be added: sides add only when the squares match. (2) **Scaling a square patch** by a whole number and what happens to its side. |
| **RAD.3** — rationalise; radical ↔ fractional exponents | one quantity written two ways | (1) **The same measure recorded two ways on one clay tablet** — as a root and as a power — which is a real thing a keeper would do and is exactly what the node teaches. ## Appendix — withdrawn text, kept for the record and moved out of the way

**Why this section exists, 2026-07-30.** This folder's convention is to withdraw superseded text by
quoting it inside a dated banner where it stood, rather than deleting it, so that a withdrawn claim
cannot quietly come back. **That convention is right for a decision and wrong for an instruction.**
The sections above are live instructions to an artist, and a withdrawn description sitting beside a
live one is not an audit trail — it is a trap, because the withdrawn version is the one that may get
drawn. There is precedent: the retired `gold-mastered` treatment survived in image-generation briefs
long after it was retired, and it mattered there more than in a colour table for exactly this reason.

**So the record survives here, in full and verbatim, and each section above carries one description.**
**Nothing in this appendix is current and nothing in it is to be drawn from.** Each entry names the
section it was moved out of and quotes the text as it stood. Withdrawal banners that are *not* here
are still where they were, because their live half is an instruction: §5's `RAD.2` re-authoring
record, the subtitle row's naming boxes, the closure block, and Ket's palette consequence.

**One thing this appendix deliberately does not contain: either side of the `RAD.2` conflict.** The
documents author a **sheep pen** and the delivered model measures as a **threshing circle with zero
brick**. That is an open question Evan owns, so the sheep-pen text is the **ruling of record** rather
than superseded text, the threshing-circle record is not "the old one", and **both stay exactly where
they are** with the flag intact.

### A3 — moved out of the palette section. THE TEAM LEAD ARGUED HUE WAS THE WRONG AXIS. OVERRULED BY EVAN, 2026-07-30, ON THE LEAD'S OWN CONDITION

**Kept in full and not cut, because it was a sound argument and measurement partly vindicated it.**
Verbatim:

> not in colour, and both eras are legitimately brown because both are made of earth.

**Where it was right, measured.** *"Never designed to differ in hue"* is confirmed by the ladder: at
**ΔE 10.1** era one → era two is the smallest adjacent step in the run, and the rationale withdrawn
as A2 says in terms that this was deliberate. *"The advance lives in round becoming square"* is also
real and is §3's own thesis — and **§5a measures how much of it was actually delivered**: only two of
the five permanent skill buildings read as square-cornered prisms, so the axis the lead was defending
is the one the art under-delivers. **That finding strengthens the lead's case, not Evan's**, and it is
recorded as such: if squareness were delivered everywhere, the hue argument would be weaker than it
is.

**Where it is overruled, and by whom.** The argument came with its own condition —

> if they're crisp and the transition still reads flat, the palette argument earns its place

— and **Evan is exercising exactly that clause.** He has now seen the delivered art, which is the
evidence the condition was waiting on, and his judgement is that the transition reads flat. He is the
designer and it is his call.

**Where it is answered rather than merely overruled: *both eras are legitimately brown because both
are made of earth.*** That premise is what §2's rewrite disputes, and it is the one place the lead's
reasoning does not survive contact with the material. **Era one's wall is earth; era two's is earth
mixed with the crop.** A sun-dried brick heavily tempered with chopped straw is not the colour of the
ground it was dug from, and it is not the colour of a fired brick either — which is what the
palette had it as. So the two eras are not both *"legitimately brown"* on the same reasoning: one is
the colour of the ground and the other is the colour of what grew on it, and the era whose entire
premise is agriculture is the one that should not be the colour of bare earth.

**What the lead's argument keeps, and it is most of it.** Round becoming square remains the advance
and §5a treats it as the primary axis, to the point of refusing to square the drum and the dome
because §3 authors both. Hue is being added as a second axis, not substituted for the first — and
`output/imagegen/era-02-neolithic-building-briefs/README.md`'s previous-era column states the reason
to keep it second: **a colour is the first thing an art pass can undo.**
