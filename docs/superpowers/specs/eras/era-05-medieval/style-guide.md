## 1. The era in one line, and its signature move

**One line:** a walled town crowded onto the previous city's stonework, wearing its timber
frame on the outside.

**The signature move — the frame on the outside of the wall.** Every era so far has either
hidden how its buildings stand up or had nothing to hide: era one lashed its posts bare
because there was no wall to put them in, and eras two to four buried the structure inside
mass. Here the structure is **drawn on the elevation**. Oak posts, rails and braces divide
the wall into panels, the panels are filled with lime-washed daub, and the pattern you see is
the load path — a curved brace where the frame would rack, close studding where it carries.
**Take the pattern away and the building falls down**, which is the test era four's colonnade
passed and the reason this is a signature rather than a decoration. It also survives the
distance the camera holds, because **a frame is a dark line on a pale field** and a line
survives being small where carved detail does not. The roofs do the rest: steep pitches, tile
covering more of a small building's elevation than wall does, and each storey oversailing the
one below, so the silhouette breaks at every floor rather than running clean to the eaves.

**The tally descendant: the chalked slate tally board.** Era one predicted this one exactly —
*notches on a post become a chalked slate becomes a printed ledger becomes a display panel* —
and the chalked slate lands here, in the guild workshop, on the barn wall, and propped by the
market cross. It is the first version of the mark **meant to be rubbed out**: a notch cut in a
post is a permanent claim, while a debt chalked up and wiped when it is paid is an account.
The counting did not change; the writing became temporary, which is what a market town needs.

## 2. Materials

Four, and the first of them is the era.

| Material | Read | Notes for modelling |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Oak frame with lime-washed daub infill** | The signature and the primary body: a pale off-white field divided into panels by dark structural timber | The panel is not a decorative grid, so vary it — close studding where the frame is proudest, wider panels with a curved brace elsewhere, and the members meeting at pegged joints rather than butting. Timber is riven and slightly irregular in section; the daub sits very slightly proud or shy of the frame face, never flush |
| **Coursed rubble stone in mortar** | Undercrofts, plinths, thresholds, chimney bases and the defensive work | Squared-ish stone in thick uneven joints — era four's fine level bed is gone, and the difference between the two is this era's whole relationship with its predecessor. Always the lower part of a building, so stone reads as the ground the frame stands on |
| **Clay roof tile and slate** | Every roof, and on the smaller buildings more of the elevation than the wall gets | Small units in overlapping courses, with a sagging ridge line and a visible eaves course. Steep enough that the roof plane is a face in its own right under the three-tone lighting model, not a cap |
| **Lead** | Spires, gutters, valleys, flashings, and the came holding glass | A dull grey that is the era's only cool note above ground level. **Leaded glass lives inside this row**: panes are small diamond quarries held in lead, so glass is a jewel set into a wall rather than a surface. **Never a large PANE** — how big the OPENING may be is a separate rule and is settled in the note under the absent list below |

**Explicitly absent, because they are later eras' signature moves:** the dome on a drum and
applied classical symmetry as a system, both the Renaissance's announcement and the reason
nothing here is symmetrical for its own sake; **window glass at any real size**, also
Renaissance, so the leaded quarry above is the largest glass this era owns; the iron frame and
every machine-made component, which wait for the Industrial era, so no two parts of a building
here match unless a hand made them match; and reinforced concrete, which waits for the Atomic
Age.

**What this era stops withholding: the pointed arch, the buttress, and the timber frame
expressed on the outside of the wall** — all three held back by era four, whose guide names
this era as the one it is saving them for. The arch and the buttress are one idea seen twice,
because a load taken up a steeper curve leans harder outward and has to be propped. The
exposed frame is the release that changes the island's colour, because it divides every wall.

## 3. Structural language

**Buildings stand up by being braced and propped from outside.** Era four gathered loads into
columns and spread them with a truss, worked out on the elevation as a repeated bay. Here the
load is taken up a steep curve, pushed sideways, and caught by something added to the wall for
that purpose — and the whole apparatus is visible.

- **The pointed arch.** Two curves meeting at a point, and the point is not a taste: arches of
  different spans can rise to the same height, which is what makes an irregular plan roofable.
  Openings are tall and narrow rather than wide.
- **The buttress.** A thickening of the wall that catches the outward push, stepped back in
  stages as it rises. It reads at zoom-out because it puts a real shadow on the wall.
- **Jettying.** Each storey oversailing the one below on projecting joist ends — the era's most
  useful silhouette move, and what breaks the vertical at every floor.
- **The steep pitch.** Roofs at fifty degrees and above, so the roof carries most of the height
  on anything under three storeys and the ridge is the building's dominant line.
- **The reared truss.** Cruck blades or a king post, assembled flat and stood up whole, with
  the tie beam and the blades visible at the gable end.
- **Ground cobbled, and failing.** Small rounded setts in rough fans over the previous era's
  sinking slabs, with the gutter down the middle of a lane rather than at its edges.

**Heights are stated as an order, and this guide deliberately gives no world-unit targets.**
Every stage is contain-fit to its plot by **one uniform scale**, so the same model stands at two
different heights on two different plots and a number is not something the person authoring one
can aim at. The **ranking** is what an era owes, and it survives re-authoring.

**This era's order, tallest first:** the bell tower, and its lead spire makes the gap over
everything else wider than it was in era four; then the chapter house and the guildhall,
effectively level with one another, which is the first time the landmark is as tall as the
hall; then the jettied row; then the tithe barn, where two-thirds of the elevation is roof;
then the guild workshop; and the kitchen garden lowest.

## 4. Ornament and visible technology

**Ornament level: moderate, and almost all of it is the frame.** The panel pattern is the
decoration and it arrives free with the structure, so the budget is spent before anything is
applied. What gets added is thin and profiled: a carved bargeboard on a gable, a moulded string
course where the jetty projects, a chamfered doorway with a pointed head, crested ridge tiles.
Where a building needs to look more important it gets **a steeper roof or another jetty**,
never more surface. Leave out tracery beyond a two-light head, foliage carving, figures,
pargeting and painted texts: all period-correct, none of it legible at zoom-out. **Spend on the
ridge line and the jetty shadow instead**, because a break in the silhouette reads at any
distance.

**Visible technology, and this era is unusually rich in it:** the treadwheel crane under its
shingled hood, the pole scaffold with its putlogs, the water mill with an undershot wheel on a
leat, the well-head windlass, the saw pit, the tracing floor, and the bell. **The bell is the
era's public instrument, and it strikes rather than shows** — a turret clock here has no dial
worth reading, because the read face belongs to the Industrial era's tower. Keep the
sound-making thing visible and the measuring thing hidden.

**Nothing that implies the drum, the pane, the machine-made unit or the cantilever.** Curves
are pointed or segmental, glass is small and leaded, no two components match unless somebody
made them match, and everything that projects is propped by something you can see.

## The island (D-ERA26)

The Classical shore was orderly and open. This era makes it **defended and crowded** — the
geometry survives underneath but nobody is maintaining it to a line any more.

**The step from era four, and this era is the one exception in the run: its ground changes STATE
rather than MATERIAL.** Every other advance brings a new surface. Era five brings almost none —
its ground is **era four's floor, failing** — and that is the right answer rather than a gap,
because §1 makes this era *"a walled town crowded onto the previous city's stonework"* and
`eras/README.md` records it as the first era whose own history is visibly load-bearing. **A
surface that has been used hard for three hundred years is a different surface**, and the
player reads it instantly: the floor that was to a line is no longer to a line.

**Ground.** Three states, and the interest is in how they meet.

- **Classical paving, failing in an interesting way.** Slabs **sunk, tilted and rocking**,
  corners broken off, joints opened and weeded, **ruts worn into them by cart wheels** along the
  routes, and **patches let in** where a slab went beyond saving — cobbles and rammed gravel
  filling a rectangular hole in a rectangular floor, which is the single most legible detail on
  this island because the patch has the old slab's outline round it.
- **New ground is cobbled rather than slabbed** — smaller, rounder, laid in **rough fans**, and
  the fan is what dates it: era four laid to a bond and this era lays to a pattern the setter
  makes up as he goes. A cobbled fan beside a broken slab is the two eras in one square metre.
- **Beaten earth again**, between buildings and in the back lanes, **muddy in the low spots**,
  with a **central gutter** running down the middle of the lane rather than at its sides — which
  is a real inversion of era four's kerb-and-gutter and worth drawing as one.

**The camber is gone**, and that is the mechanical reason for the mud: era four laid its floor
to shed water and nobody has re-laid it, so water now stands where the slabs sank. **Puddles
belong in the ruts and in the sunken bays**, and they are the era's ground-level tell that
something is no longer being maintained.

**Shoreline.** The ashlar wall is now the **base of a defensive wall**, heightened with rougher
coursed rubble, with a **wall-walk** and crenellations along the seaward stretch and a squat
gate tower where the harbour road enters. **The join is the point**: fine Classical ashlar for
the bottom third and coarse hurried rubble above it, with the change of coursing visible all the
way round. Below the wall the water's edge is **timber**: rows of driven piles and plank
revetment, tarred, with weed and barnacle at the waterline. Where the Classical steps survive
they are worn into hollows.

**Vegetation.** The formal rows are gone — **a few survivors stand out of line and overgrown**,
and they should be recognisable as era four's avenue with most of it missing. What replaces them
is **useful**: a walled kitchen garden in beds, an orchard behind a hedge, withy beds by the
water, **ivy taking the wall**. Trees are individual and unmanaged rather than placed, and there
are fewer of them. **Cultivation returns to the island after era four sent it away**, but as
garden rather than as field: enclosed, small, beside a building, and for a household rather than
for a settlement.

**Where boats land.** **Timber wharves** on piles, projecting further than the stone ever did,
with lifting gear — a treadwheel crane under a shingled hood — and a tide-mill wheel turning in
a channel cut through the revetment. Boats are moored two and three deep, working craft with
nets and barrels. The Classical stone quay is still in use and now looks like the oldest part of
the harbour, which it is.

**Water.** Working water. Still calm, but **busy and dirtier** — pale brown near the outfalls,
weed on the piles, a slick of sawdust and chaff around the mill. The harbour basin has silted
enough that a channel is dredged and marked with withy stakes.

**Signs of habitation at the margins.** A midden again, this time behind the wall. Stacked
timber and barrels. Drying nets on frames. A well-head with a windlass. Fish traps in the mill
channel. Smoke everywhere, low down. **A chalked slate propped by the market cross**, which is
§1's tally descendant meeting the ground.

**Against the roads (D-ERA30).** The relation inverts era four's, and the inversion is the
advance. Era four's road was the same stone as the ground with a **kerb** to separate it; era
five's road is **the ground everybody has worn**, with **no kerb at all** and a **gutter down its
middle**. So a route here reads as *the most damaged part of the floor* rather than as a
constructed band — ruts, patches and standing water concentrate along it. Whoever writes era
five's surfacing row should reach for **rut and patch** as courses rather than for a new
material, since the era genuinely has none. **This is the one era where the road is legitimately
a state of the ground rather than a thing laid on it**, and the two documents agreeing on that
is what keeps them from contradicting.

**What the era's own absent list rules out of the ground.** §2 withholds the **dome on a drum**
and **applied classical symmetry as a system**, **window glass at any real size**, the **iron
frame and every machine-made component**, and **reinforced concrete**. Two of those bite hard
here, and one of them is the most useful instruction in this section:

- **No machine-made component, so nothing on this ground matches anything else.** *"No two parts
  of a building here match unless a hand made them match"* — apply it to the floor. **No two
  cobbles are the same size**, the fans are not the same radius, the patches are not the same
  shape, and the kitchen-garden beds are not the same rectangle. **This is what stops the
  cobbling reading as era seven's setts**, which are the same idea done by a machine two eras
  later, and it is the discipline that keeps 5 → 7 a real advance.
- **No iron doing a FRAME's job**, so **no iron grating over the gutter, no iron drain and no
  iron shoe on the wharf piles.** Everything on the timber wharf is pegged, lashed or wedged, and
  the gutter is an open stone channel or a scoop in the earth. *(Iron as a smith's product — a
  nail, a hinge, a crane hook — is not what §2 withholds; what waits for the Industrial era is
  the **iron frame** and the machine-made part. Keep iron **hand-forged, in tension, small in
  section, and never a frame**.)*

  > **AMENDED 2026-08-01 — *"structural nowhere"* was wider than the release it protects, and
  > *"no iron tie"* is struck with it.** The bullet read *"**No structural iron**"* and closed
  > *"Keep iron small, hand-made and structural nowhere"*, which forbids the one structural use
  > this period is famous for. **Notre-Dame's iron staples date to the 1160s** — inside this era,
  > not before it — and the contemporary reason for choosing iron there was that it **"could be
  > more easily concealed"**; Italian *catene* across arch springings are standard practice in the
  > centuries this era and era six cover. So a hand-forged tie is not a thing era seven releases,

  > width of the tie. **The rule is now stated by ROLE, which is what the era actually cares
  > about:** era seven's arrival is iron **carrying the building** — the column, the truss, the

  > smith's piece, worked in tension, thin, and normally bedded out of sight. **Nothing visible

  > six's island section states the same reasoning against the drain grate — hand-made against
  > machine-made — and its §2 now carries the tie under a matching note.
- **No applied symmetry**, which reaches the ground too: nothing on this island is laid out
  about an axis. **Lanes bend.**

**Contrast against the buildings.** Body hue **lime-washed daub `#9D9C8C`** with a **dark oak and
slate accent `#5E4A3A`**, standing on grey broken paving, brown mud and grey cobble. **This era
is safe and it is the last easy one**, for a reason specific to its signature: §1 makes the frame
*"a dark line on a pale field"*, so every building carries its own high-contrast internal
pattern and needs no help from the ground to be found. **What the ground must not do is get as
dark as the oak** — keep the mud brown rather than black, or the frame lines stop reading against
it at the base of the wall.

**The accumulating city is still comfortable and this is the last era that can be said of**, but
the reason is no longer that all five are warm. Era one's terracotta, era three's ochre and era
five's own lime-wash sit at L\* 62.9, 48.0 and 64.0; era two's straw-tempered brick and era
four's limestone sit at 79.1 and 80.7. **So by era five the city is already two families, a
mid-toned warm one and a pale one**, and what keeps it legible is that every cross-family pair
is 13.3 to 27.6 ΔE76 apart in situ rather than that they are alike. The tightest of the six is
era five's own lime-wash against era four's limestone, at 13.34 over a floor of 6.72. **From
era six the run adds a third pale body, and
from era seven it adds dark-on-dark**, so era five is the high-water mark for accumulated
legibility rather than a case anybody needs to solve.

**The read:** a walled harbour town living on and inside the previous era's stonework, using it
rather than respecting it. The first era where the island's own history is visibly load-bearing.

## 6. The three construction stages

All twenty-six buildings get three models each. The stage names are fixed in code
(`buildingAssets.ts:41`), and the era's job is to make the sequence read as *this era's* way
of building — which here means the curves are drawn at full size on the ground and the roof is
built lying down.

| Stage | Engine states | Medieval read |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **`site`** | `not_started`, `preview` | A levelled platform with **a tracing floor** on it: a slab of plaster with full-size curves scribed into its surface, arch heads and rib profiles among them, some scribed across others. Beside it a stack of green oak, sawn and stickered, and a saw pit with the sawn dust round it. Flat, ankle-height at most |
| **`construction`** | `teaching`, `practice` | **Pole scaffold lashed to the wall with the putlog holes left open in the masonry**, a **treadwheel crane under a shingled hood standing on the wall head**, and the roof trusses **assembled flat on the ground and reared whole** — one standing, one going up on ropes, the rest still lying in the mud |
| **`built`** | `unfinished`, `complete`, full mastery | The finished building as described in §5 and in the roster. `advanced` (mastery ≥ 2) and full mastery both reuse this model — there is no separate gilded version, and mastery rides above it as stars (`buildingAssets.ts:28-31`) |

**The reared truss is the era's unmistakable image**, and the only stage in the run that shows a
building's largest single piece lying on the ground before it is part of the building. It works
because it is legible from above, close to the angle the camera holds: a triangle flat in the
mud beside a triangle standing on the wall head is a sequence that needs no explaining, and no
earlier era can produce it, because no earlier era assembles a roof horizontally.

**The putlog holes are the dating device, and they survive into the built stage.** Era four let
its scaffold into the wall the same way; the difference is that **this era does not go back and
fill the holes.** A finished wall keeps a regular line of small dark squares under its eaves — a
permanent record of how it was built, for the cost of one row of shadow. Era one drove stakes,
era two laid green bricks out to dry, era three hung a plumb-bob, era four scratched the plan
full size on a paved slab, and here the drawing goes onto a plaster floor and stays there under
the next one.

## Lesson framings

**What this section is.** The era folder carries lesson framing (team lead, 2026-07-29). This

**Three rules the era still imposes.**

1. **The framing comes from the era's WORLD, not the node's BUILDING** (D-ERA8) — and §4 above
   flags this era as the one that needs the reminder most, because tithes and shares map so
   neatly onto division that it is tempting to put a fraction on a façade.
2. **§2's "Explicitly absent" list is a hard boundary.** No dome on a drum, no applied classical
   symmetry, **no window glass at any real size**, no iron frame, no machine-made component, no
   reinforced concrete.
3. **Theming follows the ERA.** `RAT.5` is the exception that proves it: it is a D5 node, it is
   authored in this era's voice, and it now belongs to the Renaissance.

**The era's context palette — six reusable settings, from this guide's own props:** the **guild
hall** and its bench; the **tithe barn** and the shares it holds; the **market arcade** under the
jetty; the **walled garden** with its beds and paths; the **tracing floor** where a curve is
scribed at full size; and the **water mill** with its leat and sacks.

| Node | The mathematics | Medieval framings (2 to 3 per node) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **RAT.1** — simplify rational expressions; excluded values | factor, cancel, name what is forbidden | (1) **A share reduced to its plainest terms** — the guild records "six of eighteen" as "one of three", and the reduction is the clerk's whole job. (2) **A measure the reckoning forbids**: dividing a load among *no* members is not a small share, it is not a question, and the excluded value is where the warden stops. (3) **Two tallies over a common stick**, cancelling the notches that appear on both. |
| **RAT.2** — multiply and divide rational expressions | scaling a ratio; dividing by a fraction | (1) **Scaling a recipe or a batch** up from one barrel to the year's brewing. (2) **Splitting a tithe into portions** — dividing by a share is multiplying by its reciprocal, which the barn does physically when it measures out. (3) **Converting between measures** by multiplying through a ratio of units, so like measures cancel. |
| **RAT.3** — add and subtract rational expressions | the common denominator | (1) **Two members' shares of different-sized loads**, which cannot be added until both are expressed over the same measure. (2) **What is left after a tithe and a toll** are each taken, as a subtraction over a common measure. (3) **Two streams into one leat**, each a fraction of a different pond. |
| **RAT.4** — complex and nested rational expressions | a fraction whose parts are fractions | (1) **A share of a share** — a member's portion of a bench's portion of the whole, which the tally records as one nested reckoning. (2) **A rate of a rate**, cleared by expressing both inner measures over one outer one. |
| **RAT.6** — solve rational equations; reject extraneous roots | solving, then discarding the impossible | (1) **How much of the second load makes the mix come right** — a mixture equation the barn can actually pose. (2) **Two workers and one job**, the combined-rate reckoning, in a guild that prices work by the day. (3) **The answer the reckoning allows and the craft forbids** — a negative measure or a divisor the recipe excludes — which is where rejecting the extraneous root is a decision and not a formality. |

**Closed on 2026-07-29, and how.**

- **The walled kitchen garden IS the yard for this era, and the enclosure is the point.** The
  chain asks for *open ground deliberately kept open*, and the question was whether walling it
  breaks that. It does not: the chain's property is that the ground is **unbuilt and kept that
  way on purpose**, not that it is unbounded, and a walled town is precisely a place where
  keeping ground open takes a wall to defend it. The alternative this guide already named —
  unwalled open ground inside a walled town — reads as land nobody has claimed, which is the
  failure the chain exists to prevent. So: **walled, with beds and paths visible over the wall
  from the camera's angle**, and the wall low enough that the openness is legible at zoom-out.
- **Footprints for the five new nodes are decided**, since INV-ERA-1 fixes them forever.
  **RAT.2 (store) and RAT.6 (dwelling) take the long plot; RAT.1 (workshop), RAT.3 (hall) and
  RAT.4 (tower) take the square full plot.** A tithe barn is a long building by definition — two
  opposed cart doors with a threshing bay between them is a plan you walk through end to end —
  and a jettied row of three narrow houses is a street frontage, which is the long plot's own
  shape. The tower house takes the square plot because era one's measurement showed a smaller
  plot makes a tower *shorter*, not more slender.
  **RATIFIED 2026-07-29, so the five are frozen** — a trailing "Owner: Evan" line survived the
  closure pass on this item and read as though the decision above were still a proposal.
